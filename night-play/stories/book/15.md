# The Wrong End of the Rink

I wake to somebody knocking on a metal door and spend several seconds trying to remember which side I'm on.

Then I recognise the annex storeroom. Leila has made a bed from two coats and a camping mat Dennis found in his car. Julian is asleep in the chair, his bandaged hand supported by a rolled towel. Miso occupies the warm patch under the radiator.

The knocking is Dennis. He has brought porridge, four plastic spoons and a large piece of paper on which he has written EMERGENCY CONTACTS in capital letters.

“Somebody ought to know where everybody is,” he says.

I eat the porridge. It tastes of the inside of the cardboard tub. When I check my phone, Imani has sent the rink address with no explanation.

Leila sees me put my boots on.

“Where?”

“Rink.”

She looks at the clock, then at the bell cupboard. The holding mark has been renewed so many times that white dust covers the hinges.

“Take your phone.”

“I was planning to.”

“And don't—”

She stops. I wait.

“Tell me when you get there.”

I nod. It is a smaller request. I can manage it.

The rink smells of old rubber and cold air. Parents lean against the barrier holding paper cups. Someone is arguing about a helmet. Through the glass, skaters move between cones while a coach points at something I can't identify.

Imani is near the goal, wearing a helmet with a white scratch across the front. I hadn't realised how much equipment she needs to stand there. Her gloves look enormous. The substitute stick is dark green, with tape peeling from the shaft.

Her dad spots me and lifts his chin in greeting.

“Your aunt get the car sorted?”

“Mostly.”

“Good.”

He watches Imani stop a shot, then says, “She nervous?”

“I don't know.”

“Won't tell me.”

I have no advice for him. He seems content not to receive any.

For twenty minutes nothing supernatural happens. Nobody's reflection moves incorrectly. The scoreboard displays a sensible number. A child drops a mitten down the gap behind a bench and becomes much more upset than the situation seems to warrant, until I remember I don't know what else happened to them today.

Imani misses a shot. Her dad shifts his weight. I glance at him, but he is smiling slightly.

“Too far out,” he says, to himself.

I watch the next shot closely enough to see what he means. The puck goes left, Imani moves, the glove closes. Her dad taps the barrier with two fingers. She doesn't look over.

My phone vibrates.

Julian: Found a maintenance entry. Maybe a way around the witness circuit. Need original operator manual.

A second message follows.

Might be in the flat. Don't go without Leila.

I look at the rink doors. I could leave now and be at the flat before the session ends. I know where Mum kept her manuals. Imani wouldn't notice until she came off the ice.

On the far side of the glass, she looks towards the benches. Her helmet turns just enough for me to know she's seen me.

I put the phone away.

Afterwards, she takes off her helmet and runs a hand through flattened hair. There is a red mark across her forehead where the padding sat.

“You came.”

“You sent an address.”

“People ignore addresses all the time.”

I think of the notebook and decide not to turn that into an observation.

Her dad goes to fetch the car. She sits beside me and starts undoing the fastening on a shin pad.

“How'd it go?” I ask.

“I don't know. They don't tell you there.”

“You saved that one.”

“I saved several.”

“The one near the end.”

She looks at me. Then she laughs, a quick tired sound.

“Thanks for the detailed analysis.”

I show her Julian's messages. The smile leaves her face, but she doesn't stop undoing the fastening.

“You waited?”

“For once.”

“You can stop making everything a confession. I just asked.”

She pulls the pad free. Under it, her leg is bruised along the shin. I had been thinking of her as the person who arrived carrying a weapon. Looking at the marks, I begin to understand how much work came before she picked it up.

Her dad brings the car around. Imani makes me sit in front because her bag occupies most of the back seat. As her dad drives towards the annex, the heater blows too hot on my wet boots.

“What's wrong with the cat?” her dad asks suddenly.

I look at him.

“The black one. I saw it at the window yesterday. Didn't move for half an hour.”

I consider possible answers. “He's waiting for somebody.”

Her dad nods as if that makes sense.

Outside the annex, he waits until Imani and I reach the door. She turns back and waves. He gives the short two-finger gesture I saw at the rink, then drives away.

Inside, Julian has covered an entire sheet with the same connector drawing. This time he has labelled the missing part.

SECOND READER. INDEPENDENT ACKNOWLEDGEMENT.

I put my notebook beside it.

“Let's go home,” I say. “Properly. All of us.”
