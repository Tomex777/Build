# The Annex

The pin Leila sends takes me to a brick building behind a locked vehicle gate. There is no sign saying museum annex, no golden mask on the door, nothing to explain why my aunt knows how to get in. A green wheelie bin lies on its side beside a pile of damp cardboard.

Imani presses the intercom.

A man answers, “Deliveries after eight.”

“Leila sent us.”

Nothing happens. I can hear a radio playing on the other side, followed by the scrape of a chair. Then the gate releases with a buzz.

“Nice security,” I say.

“The gate,” the man tells me through the intercom, “is on camera.”

I stop talking to the gate.

Inside the service entrance, the air smells of radiator dust and cardboard. A security officer sits behind a desk with a half-completed crossword. He has written the same answer in two different places and is frowning at one of them.

“She's downstairs. Don't touch anything. Cat stays out.”

The cat walks between his shoes.

He looks down at it. “You again.”

“Evening, Dennis.”

Dennis removes his glasses, rubs the bridge of his nose and puts them back on. “I've told them about this. They still won't put it in the incident form.”

Imani and I follow the cat before Dennis remembers we were supposed to be doing something ordinary.

The basement stairs end at a fire door propped open with a crate of bottled water. Leila stands beyond it in her navy scrubs, coat unbuttoned, her hair escaping its clip. She has one of the hospital's small cardboard cups in her hand. When she sees me she puts it down without finding a surface. It falls and spills coffee across the floor.

She hugs me first. I don't decide whether to let her. Her arms close around my shoulders and my face presses into the clean hospital smell of her sleeve, and for a second I stop holding myself upright.

Then she takes my elbow.

“Sit.”

“I'm fine.”

“Sit while being fine.”

She cleans the cut with something from her bag. It hurts more than I expect. Imani leans her stick against the table and watches the door. The cat starts washing its chest as if it has done all the work available.

“You knew,” I say.

Leila peels the backing off a dressing.

“About that thing in the flat. You said you knew.”

“I knew it might try.”

The cat stops washing. Imani looks at me, then discreetly studies a shelf of archive boxes.

“How long?”

Leila presses the dressing down too firmly. I flinch, and she loosens her grip.

“Since your mum went missing.”

There is a notice on the wall asking staff to separate paper and plastic waste. I read it three times while she tells me that my mother studied things she couldn't put in published papers. That the little brick building stores equipment which isn't part of the museum's public collection. That some of the work happened here, after hours, with permission from people who knew enough to sign the right forms and not enough to be useful now.

“You let the police look for her.”

“Yes.”

“You let me go with them.”

“{{firstName}}.”

“I showed them her favourite coffee place.”

Leila puts the unused half of the dressing packet into her pocket. “I remember.”

I pull my arm away. “Were you ever going to tell me?”

She looks towards Imani. I hate her for that small glance, for measuring how much can be said in front of somebody else.

Imani picks up her stick. “There's a vending machine upstairs. I'm going to call my dad.”

Nobody asks her to leave. She goes anyway, letting the fire door close behind her with a gentle click.

Leila waits until it has shut. “I didn't know whether Nadia was alive.”

“But you knew where she'd gone.”

“I knew the name of a place. I couldn't reach it.”

“You could have told me that.”

“Yes,” she says.

The answer is so plain that for a moment I have nothing to put against it. I had prepared for excuses. I've spent eleven months listening to people explain how little information they can give me.

I put the notebook on the table. Her face changes when she sees it.

“Where did you find that?”

“In the drawer. Where you keep the menus.”

“It wasn't there.”

“I know.”

She sits down opposite me. Her hand hovers above the cover without touching it. I see a faint white scar across the inside of her wrist, one I asked about last summer. She said she'd burned herself with steam.

The cat jumps onto the table.

“He needs the diagram,” it says.

Leila's expression hardens. “You need to tell me how you got out.”

“I walked.”

“You don't walk.”

“I've been practising.”

Something taps against a cupboard door on the far wall. The cat lowers itself until its belly touches the table. Leila gets up at once.

“Stay there.”

Of course I don't.

Inside the cupboard is an old brass bell, the kind with a little button on top. It sits on a saucer of salt. Beside it lie a battery recorder, a length of cut telephone cable and a photograph of a girl in a yellow raincoat. Somebody has folded the photograph so tightly that a white line runs across the girl's face.

The bell's button pushes down.

Leila catches it before it can release.

A voice comes from the recorder, although its screen is off. I recognise the small voice from the interrupted call.

“Dad says we're not ready.”

The cat backs into my leg.

Leila holds the bell so tightly her hand shakes. “{{firstName}}. Open the notebook. First blue tab.”

I do. Under the tab is a drawing of a circle crossed by three short lines. One of the lines is broken. Beneath it, Mum has written: Temporary hold. Seven minutes maximum. Never use on living tissue.

I show Leila.

“I know what it looks like. Draw it.”

“With what?”

She nods at a box on the shelf. Inside are sticks of white chalk wrapped in tissue. My fingers close around one and come away dusty.

I copy the circle on the cupboard's inside wall. The chalk squeaks. Nothing happens. Leila makes a small sound through her teeth.

“Close the end. It's open at the bottom.”

I repair the gap.

The bell stops moving. Leila takes her hand away. A red mark has appeared across her palm.

On the table, my phone lights up.

A message from an unknown number reads: “Your mother is tired. We should discuss this before somebody makes a mistake.”

The name beneath it is Adrian Voss.
