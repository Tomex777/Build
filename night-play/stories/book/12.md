# What Julian Cannot Remember

The correction doesn't make the lie disappear. Thoth rules a narrow line through it, leaving every letter legible, and attaches a reference number to the evidence.

“People will still be able to see it?” I ask.

“They need to see what was changed.”

He writes a second address beneath the first. The marks look simple until I try to copy them. One seems to belong behind another, although they're both on the same page.

Julian turns my notebook sideways.

“Write the order separately.”

I make six small numbered boxes. Thoth watches me fill them.

“Take the witness receipt,” he says. “You may challenge a delegated authority once. It will not hold the door open for you. It will make the door ask the question it was told to skip.”

I fold the receipt along its printed line. It is longer than my pocket, so I fold it twice more. Nobody says anything about the untidy result.

On the way out, Imani buys a bottle of water from the laundrette. Ordinary water, from an ordinary fridge, with an ordinary price she objects to before paying it. Julian helps carry the trolley up the stairs. The owner says, “Finally,” and gives him a packet of biscuits.

We sit on the laundrette's front step and pass the biscuits between us. It is nearly six. The sky has the flat purple colour it gets before snow, and the bus shelter across the road is crowded with people trying not to touch.

Julian takes one biscuit, then breaks it and puts half back.

“Ruth doesn't know,” he says.

I turn towards him. He keeps looking at the road.

“About the wedding. I let her think I was drunk. The photographer sent me the pictures. I know where everyone stood. I can describe the cake.”

“Doesn't that help?”

“It makes me better at lying about it.”

The laundrette door opens behind me. Warm air and the smell of soap spill over the steps. A woman carries a bag past me without looking down.

Imani asks, “What did you need the address book for?”

Julian rubs the biscuit crumbs off his hand.

“Nadia. After she disappeared. I thought I could find her before anybody noticed how much I didn't know.”

I hadn't expected that. I thought he would say a rare book, or a promotion, or a door he shouldn't have tried. Something that would make the price easier to judge.

“I didn't know you,” he adds. “It wasn't for you.”

“Okay.”

“She took me seriously. At work. That's all.”

He says that's all in a tone that makes Imani stop asking questions.

Miso is crouched under the step, watching a delivery cyclist fix a dropped chain. His collar has appeared around his neck. I haven't seen it before. Thin black leather, too tight at the throat, with a brass plate tucked under the fur.

I remember what Voss said.

“Whose name keeps it closed?”

The cat goes still.

Julian looks up sharply. “Don't.”

“Voss told me to ask.”

“Voss tells everybody something true when he wants them to stop noticing the rest.”

Miso stands and walks towards the alley. I get up after him.

“I wasn't—”

He moves faster. I follow him around the corner, leaving Imani and Julian on the step.

At the end of the alley, he turns.

“Yes,” he says. “I worked for him.”

“Doing what?”

“Opening doors. Carrying messages. Finding people he said were lost.”

“My mother?”

The question hangs there long enough to answer itself.

I sit back on a low wall. My elbow throbs where the dressing pulls against the cut.

“You took her there.”

“She asked me to.”

“Did she know you worked for him?”

“No.”

A rubbish bag shifts in the wind. Miso watches it instead of me.

I have to put my hands somewhere. I push them under my arms.

“You could have told me.”

“I could.”

“Everyone keeps saying that after.”

He raises his head. Under the brass plate, his fur has worn away in a narrow strip.

“I don't get to tell every truth I know,” he says. “I can tell you this one because you brought it out of his mouth and put it into yours. Ask me something else about him and see what happens.”

I don't. I'm not willing to test the collar by hurting him just to see whether he's honest.

Imani appears at the corner. She stops when she sees our faces.

“Julian's got a message from Leila. Bell's getting worse. She says if we're using that address, we go now.”

Miso walks past her without speaking. I wait until he has gone, then show Imani the photograph I copied at the gallery. The list of numbers on the back. The part I hadn't told anybody about because I wanted to be the person with something useful.

She studies the page. Then she looks at me.

“You were going to keep this?”

“I was checking it.”

“With who?”

I don't answer.

She gives the notebook back carefully, as if she'd rather not be holding it.

“At least let me know what I'm walking into,” she says.

I put the page where everyone can see it when I return to the step.
