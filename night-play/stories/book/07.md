# Mrs. Bell's Last Message

The collection schedule gives me a deadline and no address. I turn the paper over as if the missing information might be on the back.

Julian takes it away before I wear a hole through it.

“We have to trace an earlier call. Same receiving point, older sender. There'll be an acknowledgement.”

“An address.”

“If the operator kept records.”

“Did she?”

He raises one eyebrow at the notebook in my hand.

Miso leads me to a booth with a cracked pane. Behind the glass, a telephone sits beside a sewing basket. An empty chair faces a window full of afternoon sunshine. It is bright enough to make my eyes hurt after the long night.

“This was Celia Bell's,” Julian says. “A volunteer operator. She died before the connection broke. Her channel's been replaying because the last message never reached its destination.”

“Where's Celia?”

“Gone on. This is what she left.”

He doesn't offer a simpler explanation. I am beginning to appreciate that about him.

The door opens into a flat full of warm air and the smell of something baking. A radio plays softly in the kitchen. There are birthday cards on the mantelpiece, several months of them displayed together. On the table beside the telephone lies an envelope addressed to a man called Martin.

A woman in a green cardigan sits by the window. When I come in, she looks up and smiles.

“Are you from the pharmacy?”

“No,” I say.

“I rang yesterday. They said they'd send somebody.”

Imani stays in the doorway. Miso sniffs the woman's shoes, then moves to the telephone.

The woman doesn't react to him. She repeats her question.

“Are you from the pharmacy?”

I understand then, a little. The room is starting over. The radio plays the same two notes. The kitchen clock clicks back from twelve to eleven.

On the envelope, the ink of Martin's name runs down the paper.

Julian points towards a cabinet behind the woman's chair. “Routing book. We need the entries for last March.”

I could walk around her. She isn't watching me; her gaze is fixed on the door, waiting for a person who must be taking a long time. But her hand rests on the cabinet's drawer, and when Julian tries to open it she grips the handle.

“Private papers.”

“We need to help someone.”

“Private papers,” she says again.

I look at the letter. The envelope hasn't been sealed. A faint rectangle marks the place where a stamp should be.

“Who is Martin?” I ask.

The radio stops.

“My son.”

“Can we deliver your letter?”

For the first time her eyes follow me. They are a cloudy blue, with the red rims of someone who hasn't slept well.

“He doesn't answer the phone.”

“Do you have his address?”

She gestures to the envelope. The street name has almost disappeared. I write it down before it can go entirely.

Julian shifts his weight. “{{firstName}}. We can't start taking on every—”

“I said can we. I haven't said I will.”

Mrs. Bell looks towards the kitchen. A timer sounds once and dies.

“I told him not to come,” she says. “He was driving in that weather. I said it could wait until Tuesday.”

I sit on the edge of the sofa. The cushion has a dent where someone must have sat for years.

“Did he come?”

“No.”

She looks relieved, and then unhappy, and then relieved again. The room doesn't seem able to decide which part to keep.

Imani takes out her phone. There is no signal, but she types the address into a note.

“We can find out whether he still lives there,” she says. “That's a thing we can actually promise.”

Mrs. Bell releases the drawer.

Inside, the routing book is an ordinary ledger with ruled columns. Most of the entries are numbers. In the margin beside Nadia {{surname}}, a small cross has been drawn through the return line. Under it, my mother's writing says: Received at lower station. Witness present: A. Voss. Ask why he didn't sign.

I photograph the page. On my screen, the photograph shows only a blank ledger.

“Pen,” Julian says, passing me one. “Paper holds. Cameras copy the wrong layer.”

I write the entry in my notebook, including the little cross and the exact position of the unsigned box.

The woman reaches for the letter. Her fingers go through it.

“Oh,” she says.

The afternoon light thins.

Miso stands beside the door, tail rigid. “We should leave.”

The room has begun to tilt. Cups slide across the mantelpiece. Imani catches my sleeve as the sofa shifts away from the wall. I take the envelope with my other hand and pull back towards the booth.

Mrs. Bell has stopped looking at me. She is watching the window.

“Tell him it wasn't because I didn't want him,” she says.

I cannot tell whether she's asking me or remembering a conversation she already had.

Back in the exchange room, the glass pane cracks from top to bottom. The flat goes dark. My watch says six minutes and forty-eight seconds have passed.

I still have the letter.

Julian closes the booth door carefully.

“You're going to need to decide,” he says, “which promises fit in two days.”

I put the envelope inside the notebook, where the address won't rub away.
