# Adrian's Offer

Voss is waiting outside the annex when you return. He stands under the security light with his hands visible and his phone on the pavement beside him.

Dennis has locked the gate. Leila stands inside it with the metal case at her feet.

“I didn't let him in,” she says.

“I can see.”

Voss looks at Wren. For a moment he seems unable to place her. Then his eyes find the borrowed coat.

“Eva.”

“Wren,” she says.

He looks at you. “What have you done?”

“She picked a name.”

“You don't know what she's carrying.”

“Three thousand four hundred and eleven responses,” Julian says. “At least. Your load count was wrong.”

Voss turns towards him. The courtesy drains away.

“That's an instantaneous count. Not an accumulation. Some will be duplicates.”

“And some won't.”

He looks back at the gate. Leila doesn't move.

For a while the two engineers argue through the bars. Voss is better informed about the current load. Julian has the original calibration. Neither has enough on his own, and both know it. You listen until their sentences become so specific that the fear underneath them almost disappears.

Then Voss says, “You need me.”

Leila picks up his phone with a cloth and passes it through the gate.

“We need information you have. That's different from giving you control.”

He takes the phone. His hand shakes once before he closes it around the case.

You show him the transcript of Eva's first call.

He reads the first line and stops.

“I know what it says.”

“Then you know she said she had to go.”

“Don't.”

“Did you ever find the note under the bowl?”

He looks at you as if you have touched him without permission.

“My wife found it.”

“What did it say?”

“That isn't yours.”

“No,” you say. “It isn't.”

You put the transcript away. You hadn't meant to give him that answer. It leaves you both with the strange discomfort of an argument that doesn't finish in the expected place.

Wren comes to the gate. She stands where Voss can see her face clearly.

“I remember the calls,” she says. “I don't remember the kitchen. I know the words because you kept playing them.”

Voss puts one hand on the bars. “You don't have to remember everything at once.”

“I don't want her things.”

“You liked the snow globe.”

“I liked the water moving.”

He looks away.

Behind him, a bus goes past with most of its seats empty. The driver waits at the red light even though there is no traffic. You find yourself staring at the patient, ordinary obedience of it.

Leila opens the gate only far enough to put a chair outside.

“If you give us the revised load sequence, we'll record your contribution. Nobody will pretend you didn't help. You won't operate the reader.”

“And Nadia?”

“We bring her out.”

“The thing inside won't let you close the line.”

Wren steps back from the gate.

You see her face begin to change, and realise she is trying to find one he will listen to. You put your notebook down before your hands can become part of another instruction.

“Wren,” you say, quietly. “You don't have to.”

She looks at you. The face settles.

Voss sits in the chair. For the next twenty minutes he dictates numbers to Julian. He stops twice to correct a figure and once to insist that a particular connection must remain open. Julian writes the correction down and refuses the open connection.

When the sequence is finished, Leila checks it against the original.

“You left out the limit.”

Voss says nothing.

“The return window,” she says. “How long?”

“Seven minutes.”

“Under current load.”

He looks towards Wren, who is sitting on the inside step with Miso beside her.

“Four,” he says.

Julian crosses out seven.

You read the new figure. Four minutes to hold the broken connections, ask the questions the bypass removed, release your mother and bring everybody through. After two days of searching, the answer fits inside a number you can count on one hand.

Voss gets up.

“I can hold the master line. From inside.”

Leila looks at you. This time she doesn't decide in advance which answer you are allowed to hear.

You look at Voss's hands. No cable burns. No grey skin. He has found ways to stay outside the machinery he built.

“If you come,” you say, “you don't get a private channel.”

He nods, reluctantly.

“And if Wren says no, she means no.”

He looks as if he might argue about the name again. Then he picks his phone up from the chair and puts it in his pocket.

“All right.”

You do not mistake the words for a guarantee. Julian writes them down anyway.
