# The Last Call

Mum borrows a pen from the nurse and draws the return circuit on the back of a hospital menu. The soup choices get in the way. She works around them.

“The first connection kept a route back to where the claim began. It couldn't close while the receiving instruction was still active.”

“You're saying I called myself.”

“I'm saying you can. I don't know whether you have to.”

The distinction feels like something I learned on the stairs: an open road doesn't decide whether I use it.

I look at the draft call. Friday, 02:13. My kitchen. The chair at the table. The version of me who hasn't yet learned which details will become important.

I could tell myself everything. The bell, the notebook, the false signature. I try to imagine receiving that list from an unknown caller and remember how close I came to turning the phone off. Maybe a message can only carry what the person on the other end can hold.

Mum hands back the phone. She doesn't tell me what to choose.

I call.

For one ring I hear nothing. Then the line opens to the sound of my own frightened breathing.

I press my free hand against the hospital chair.

“Whatever happens,” I say, “do not open the door.”

At the other end, a chair scrapes across laminate.

“How do I stop it?”

My own voice, younger by two days and a great deal else.

“Blue notebook. Kitchen drawer.”

“What?”

“Mum's notebook. Get it. And {{firstName}}—”

The connection closes.

I sit with the phone still against my ear. Mum's good hand rests on my sleeve.

“What were you going to say?” she asks.

I look at the hospital menu with its impossible diagram. The floor outside the curtain. My aunt returning with tea she will insist somebody drinks before it gets cold.

“I don't know. Something useful.”

Mum rubs her thumb across the worn bird patch on my coat.

“You got the notebook,” she says.

Three weeks later, the flat contains more people than it has chairs. Julian brings a folding one from the archive, which he says should not leave the building and has therefore labelled with the building's name in case that helps.

Leila makes dinner. Mum tries to help and is told to sit down. They argue for four minutes about whether chopping one onion counts as rest. I let them. I hadn't realised I missed being annoyed by both of them at once.

Voss is under investigation. There are statements about Nadia's disappearance, equipment records, unsafe access and financial arrangements nobody in my kitchen is qualified to explain. His wife has asked for the original recording of Eva's call. Julian sends a copy through a solicitor. I don't hear what she says when she receives it. I don't need to.

Mum goes to appointments. Some days her hand hurts too much to hold a pen. Some nights she knocks on my bedroom door to make sure I'm there, then pretends she needed to borrow a charger. I stop pretending to believe her and let her sit for a while.

Leila and I do not repair everything in one conversation. She starts telling me when she doesn't know something. I start telling her before leaving the building. It is inconvenient for both of us. We keep doing it.

Wren has been living in the annex's spare room while people work out what papers the ordinary world will accept. She comes over wearing a coat that fits. She has learned to dislike olives and asks whether this is a personality or just a food opinion.

“Both, if you mention it often enough,” Imani tells her.

Julian has called his sister. He didn't get the wedding back. He told her why the photographs had become homework. I don't know how much she believed. I know he is going to her house on Sunday and bringing a cake he has been instructed not to choose himself.

Miso sleeps on my chemistry textbook. I move him only when absolutely necessary, which he defines differently from me.

After dinner, Imani stays to help clear the table. Her phone lights up with a message from the selection coach. She reads it twice, then hands me the screen without speaking.

I read the message. Look at her. She is trying very hard not to smile before I do.

“You got it.”

“Conditional. Still training.”

“You got it.”

She grins properly then. I shout for Leila, who comes in carrying a wet plate and complains that I frightened her before joining the celebration.

Later, when everyone has gone, I find a small paper bird on the fire escape. Wren made it from the blank end of an old archive form. Its wings are uneven. I bring it inside and put it beside Mum's blue notebook.

My phone rings.

Imani. She left her gloves under the chair, and she would like I to stop telling people she cried at the message, because she didn't.

I look towards the kitchen, where Mum is trying to make toast without setting off the alarm.

I answer.

THE END
