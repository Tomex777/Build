# The Last Call

Mum borrows a pen from the nurse and draws the return circuit on the back of a hospital menu. The soup choices get in the way. She works around them.

“The first connection kept a route back to where the claim began. It couldn't close while the receiving instruction was still active.”

“You're saying I called myself.”

“I'm saying you can. I don't know whether you have to.”

The distinction feels like something you learned on the stairs: an open road doesn't decide whether you use it.

You look at the draft call. Friday, 02:13. Your kitchen. The chair at the table. The version of you who hasn't yet learned which details will become important.

You could tell yourself everything. The bell, the notebook, the false signature. You try to imagine receiving that list from an unknown caller and remember how close you came to turning the phone off. Maybe a message can only carry what the person on the other end can hold.

Mum hands back the phone. She doesn't tell you what to choose.

You call.

For one ring you hear nothing. Then the line opens to the sound of your own frightened breathing.

You press your free hand against the hospital chair.

“Whatever happens,” you say, “do not open the door.”

At the other end, a chair scrapes across laminate.

“How do I stop it?”

Your own voice, younger by two days and a great deal else.

“Blue notebook. Kitchen drawer.”

“What?”

“Mum's notebook. Get it. And Sam—”

The connection closes.

You sit with the phone still against your ear. Mum's good hand rests on your sleeve.

“What were you going to say?” she asks.

You look at the hospital menu with its impossible diagram. The floor outside the curtain. Your aunt returning with tea she will insist somebody drinks before it gets cold.

“I don't know. Something useful.”

Mum rubs her thumb across the worn bird patch on your coat.

“You got the notebook,” she says.

Three weeks later, the flat contains more people than it has chairs. Julian brings a folding one from the archive, which he says should not leave the building and has therefore labelled with the building's name in case that helps.

Leila makes dinner. Mum tries to help and is told to sit down. They argue for four minutes about whether chopping one onion counts as rest. You let them. You hadn't realised you missed being annoyed by both of them at once.

Voss is under investigation. There are statements about Nadia's disappearance, equipment records, unsafe access and financial arrangements nobody in your kitchen is qualified to explain. His wife has asked for the original recording of Eva's call. Julian sends a copy through a solicitor. You don't hear what she says when she receives it. You don't need to.

Mum goes to appointments. Some days her hand hurts too much to hold a pen. Some nights she knocks on your bedroom door to make sure you're there, then pretends she needed to borrow a charger. You stop pretending to believe her and let her sit for a while.

You and Leila do not repair everything in one conversation. She starts telling you when she doesn't know something. You start telling her before leaving the building. It is inconvenient for both of you. You keep doing it.

Wren has been living in the annex's spare room while people work out what papers the ordinary world will accept. She comes over wearing a coat that fits. She has learned to dislike olives and asks whether this is a personality or just a food opinion.

“Both, if you mention it often enough,” Imani tells her.

Julian has called his sister. He didn't get the wedding back. He told her why the photographs had become homework. You don't know how much she believed. You know he is going to her house on Sunday and bringing a cake he has been instructed not to choose himself.

Miso sleeps on your chemistry textbook. You move him only when absolutely necessary, which he defines differently from you.

After dinner, Imani stays to help clear the table. Her phone lights up with a message from the selection coach. She reads it twice, then hands you the screen without speaking.

You read the message. Look at her. She is trying very hard not to smile before you do.

“You got it.”

“Conditional. Still training.”

“You got it.”

She grins properly then. You shout for Leila, who comes in carrying a wet plate and complains that you frightened her before joining the celebration.

Later, when everyone has gone, you find a small paper bird on the fire escape. Wren made it from the blank end of an old archive form. Its wings are uneven. You bring it inside and put it beside Mum's blue notebook.

Your phone rings.

Imani. She left her gloves under the chair, and she would like you to stop telling people she cried at the message, because she didn't.

You look towards the kitchen, where Mum is trying to make toast without setting off the alarm.

You answer.

THE END
