# Breakfast at Nine

Leila collects you from the service door of a building you could have walked past a hundred times. She has borrowed Dennis's car, an elderly hatchback with a child seat in the back and a packet of wet wipes in every available pocket.

“Dennis has grandchildren,” she explains when Imani asks.

“I wasn't asking about the wipes.”

“No,” Leila says. “Sorry.”

Julian sits in the front. He has to hold a cardboard box in his lap because the passenger footwell is full of additional wet wipes. Miso stays inside the box with the flap partly open. Nobody has suggested he needs to hide; he seems to prefer being luggage to being questioned.

You are home before nine. The flat looks ordinary from the doorway. The chair you wedged under the handle is back at the kitchen table. The screw from the handle sits beside your laptop. Someone has laid the chemistry worksheet neatly on top of it, although you left it on a streetcar which crossed a river under the city.

Leila checks the rooms before letting you in. She uses a little mirror from her bag, turning it towards each corner. You want to ask how she knows what to look for. You want to go straight to bed and wake up yesterday.

There is a note on the table.

You have my face in the mirror. I'd like it back.

It is written in pencil. The letters have the careful shape of someone copying them from a sign.

Miso sniffs it, sneezes and climbs onto the windowsill.

“Don't destroy it,” Julian says.

“I wasn't going to.”

“You look like you want to.”

You put the note under an empty glass.

Imani's dad arrives while Leila is making toast. He is broad-shouldered, tired, and wearing a work jacket with a rip at the cuff. He looks first at Imani, then at Leila, then at you. His eyes stop on the dressing at your elbow.

“Car trouble?” he says.

Leila turns off the toaster.

“A situation at the building. I needed Imani to help Sam get out.”

“What situation?”

“The door wouldn't open safely.”

You can see her searching for a statement that is accurate enough to survive. You used to think grown-ups had some private source of better explanations. Watching your aunt choose each word, you begin to suspect that a lot of adulthood is deciding how much trouble the next sentence will cause.

Imani's dad doesn't like the answer. He doesn't have time to stay and dislike it properly. He tells Imani to get her bag.

She says, “I need to come back later.”

“For what?”

“Helping.”

“Helping somebody else miss school?”

“Dad.”

“Selection is tomorrow. You told me that was important.”

“It is.”

“Then eat. Sleep. And answer your phone.”

She takes the toast Leila offers and wraps it in a paper towel. At the door she looks back at you, waiting for something.

You realise you haven't thanked her.

“Thanks.”

She gives a short nod. It isn't quite the response you wanted, but you aren't sure what response you earned.

After she leaves, Julian telephones his college and says he has a family emergency. He doesn't stumble over the lie. When he catches you looking, he covers the receiver.

“My manager won't write 'trapped in another realm' on a reference.”

Leila calls your school. You hear her say medical issue, family situation, expected back Monday. The office person asks a question that makes her close her eyes.

“No. We don't have an update about Nadia.”

You leave the kitchen before she can finish.

Your room smells of the orange peel you left on the desk yesterday. The bed is unmade. A clean school shirt hangs from the wardrobe handle because Leila refuses to put away clothes you are perfectly capable of putting away yourself. Everything in the room is arranged around a person who thought they knew how Friday would go.

You sit on the bed and take off your boots. Your socks have dried stiff at the ankles.

In your phone's drafts is the last message you wrote to Mum. You open it.

Leila bought the wrong cereal again. I know you'd say it's the same cereal in a different box. It isn't. You can tell.

There is no ending to the message. You always got stuck there. How do you say goodbye to an email address when you've spent months insisting you're only saying see you later?

You delete nothing. You close the draft.

On the other side of the wall, Julian and Leila are talking quietly. You can make out your name, Voss's name, the word lower. A chair scrapes. You press your face into the pillow, angry at how tired you are.

When you wake, someone has put a glass of water by the bed. The flat is silent except for Miso snoring on your clean shirt.

It is two in the afternoon.

Your phone contains a message from Imani: Don't do the important bit without telling me.

Underneath is a second message.

Seriously.

You lie there with the phone balanced on your chest. Then you reply: Haven't found the important bit yet.

The typing indicator appears immediately.

You could start by asking.
