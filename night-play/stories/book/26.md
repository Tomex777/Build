# One Dry Run

Nobody likes rehearsing an emergency. Leila says this while making everyone do it a third time.

Julian has arranged the equipment on two tables. The bell case is at one end, the second reader at the other. Between them lie paper cards representing Nadia, the waiting messages, Wren, the witness, and the operator. He has drawn a little cat on the card for Miso. Miso has been trying to sit on it ever since.

“You're not a load-bearing component,” Julian tells him.

“Good. Finally.”

Voss stands near the door. Dennis watches him from a chair, holding a mug with both hands. There is no realistic reason a retired security officer with a mug should intimidate Voss. It appears to be working anyway.

The first rehearsal fails because you close the holding mark before Imani positions the reader. The second fails because Julian changes a setting with the wrong hand and knocks the lever out of alignment. The third fails because Wren says she isn't ready and everyone else takes too long to stop.

“We stop when she says it,” you say.

Voss turns towards you.

“If we stop at the wrong time—”

“Then we need to know what stopping means before we're in there.”

You hear Leila's teaching voice in your own. It is irritating and useful.

Julian marks three stages on the table with tape. Before the first, you can withdraw. Between the first and second, the system holds temporarily. After the second, the messages have begun to separate; stopping then means choosing another way to contain them.

“What other way?” Imani asks.

“Break the bell,” Leila says. “Sever the crossing. It will get us out, but unfinished messages will lose the only route we know how to reach.”

Wren looks at her card. Her name is written in Julian's cramped hand beside the words independent receiving record.

“And me?”

“The record should keep you separate,” Julian says. “I can't promise what the shock will do.”

You appreciate the honest sentence and hate the uncertainty inside it.

Voss takes a pen and points to the operator's card. “There is one further option. A live receiver could hold the load while the rest of you disconnect. With the bypass removed, it would no longer be forced.”

Everyone looks at him.

“Yes,” he says. “I am aware.”

Mum held it for eleven months. You think about how Voss has arranged the possibility so it sounds newly reasonable now that he has said willing.

Julian puts a second card underneath the operator.

“One person. No ordinary way out. We'd have to build a new receiving point from outside.”

“We don't choose that by accident,” Imani says.

“No,” Leila agrees.

You rehearse again. This time Wren says stop halfway through, and all of you stop. Miso takes his paw off the cat card. Julian lowers the lever. Imani sets the reader down. You open the outer line of the mark without breaking the inner one.

Nothing falls. Nothing is trapped. Wren breathes out and looks almost embarrassed by her relief.

You go again. At the end, Julian checks the timer.

“Three minutes and twenty-eight seconds.”

Imani looks at the table. “Thirty-two spare.”

“Only if nobody argues,” Dennis says.

Voss does not look at him.

Leila packs the equipment. She labels everything in large letters, including the bag containing chalk, which nobody was likely to confuse with anything else. You do not tease her. You have started to understand how much fear can fit inside being organised.

Miso noses the edge of your notebook. A page you haven't read falls open. On it, Mum has sketched several jackal figures in different poses. Beneath one she has written Wepwawet, Opener of the Ways. Beside the name is a note: Ask for a road, not ownership of the road. Courtesy isn't payment. Precision matters.

You read the name aloud carefully. Miso looks towards the door.

“He may be there,” he says. “When the lower route narrows.”

“Does knowing the name help?”

“Knowing which name to use helps. Don't assume any name gives you command.”

You mark the page with a strip of blue tape. Imani does the same to the release diagram, then hands the roll back without saying anything about whether she trusts you to remember it.

Near midnight, Dennis brings the car around. Leila checks every latch on the bell case. Voss carries the equipment bag under Imani's supervision. Julian signs the archive's paper register before leaving, including an estimated return time.

You look at the blank line after his signature.

“You think they'll care?”

“I will,” he says.

You write your name under his. Your ordinary name, in your ordinary handwriting, with a pen that is running out of ink. Then you close the register and follow the others out.
