# Four Minutes

At first the reader makes no sound. You can hear the click of the outer timer through Leila's phone, the shallow breathing of the people nearest you, and a relay moving under Nadia's table.

Then every label in the room turns towards the brass box.

You are not sure labels can turn. You watch the movement happen anyway, name after name lifting its lower edge as if caught by a wind you cannot feel.

Julian puts his good hand on the lever.

“First stage.”

You close the holding mark.

The resistance is immediate. Not pain, exactly. The sense that a weight has been placed on the far end of a long tool you are trying to keep level. Your fingers tighten around the chalk. You remember the torn papers on Julian's desk. The forty-one seconds. The way breathing stopped the pieces shaking.

You breathe.

Wren lays her receiving card beside the reader. Her name appears on the blank indicator strip.

Mum starts to open her burned hand.

A cable jerks through the brass loop around her wrist. She cries out and closes her fingers again.

Julian lowers the lever. “Not yet. Something's still returning the refusal as a yes.”

Voss turns towards the master panel.

You see his hand stop over a switch he hasn't mentioned.

“What does that do?”

“Return cache.”

“Turn it off.”

“If we clear it, we lose the stored calls.”

“Turn it off.”

He looks at Wren. The reader is pulling separate voices out of her shape; faces flicker across her skin, too fast to distinguish. At the centre, the face she chose is trying to hold still.

“Eva's in there somewhere,” he says.

You want to shout. You need the breath to hold the mark.

Imani steps between him and the switch. “You heard her leave.”

Voss takes out the photograph. You hadn't realised he still had it with him. He studies Eva's face as if the answer might be written somewhere the camera failed to focus.

Leila's phone says, “Two minutes.”

Mum loosens one finger. Another cable slams against the table. The receiver count doubles.

Wren looks at you. “Too fast.”

Julian has one hand on the lever and the other held uselessly against his chest. He could slow the release, but not while he holds the reader aligned. Imani could reach him, but that would leave Voss alone at the master panel.

Your mark begins to tremble.

Miso puts one paw beside the outer circle. Not inside it. He waits until you nod, then adds his weight to the connection. The trembling eases.

You have seconds in which you can think.

The first option is to finish the separation. It needs a receiver who can remain distinct while the messages move, a route that recognises Wren, and another person to hold the reader steady. You have either earned those things or you haven't. Wanting them now won't put them in the room.

The second is to break the bell. Leila can sever the crossing from outside while Nadia still has enough strength to leave. Wren's new record may hold her shape, but the unfinished voices will be cut away. Some will never reach the people waiting for them.

The third is to take the whole receiving load yourself. Not replace Nadia under Voss's old instruction; choose a new, limited service and give the others time to disconnect. You would stay inside. They could build a way to reach you, but nobody here knows how to bring you home.

Mum sees where you are looking.

“You do not owe me that,” she says.

“I know.”

“You need to know before you choose.”

You look at her burned hand, at Julian's bandage, at the card Wren wrote her own name on. You look at Imani, who has been waiting for you to make a decision she can disagree with and still help you carry out.

Leila's phone says, “Ninety seconds.”

You put the chalk against the last open line.
