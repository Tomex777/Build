# Tea With Adrian Voss

The gallery sells landscape paintings and very expensive lamps. Through the window I can see a blue lake rendered in thick paint, with a small red canoe positioned to make the blue seem bigger. I spend a moment wondering whether the painter has ever been in a canoe before remembering that I have come to confront a man about my missing mother.

Voss is waiting at a table near the back. He gets up when I enter.

He looks ordinary. This annoys me. His coat is hung neatly on the chair. His shirt has a pale stain near one cuff. There is a loose thread on his scarf which he keeps trying to tuck out of sight.

“{{firstName}}.” He looks at Imani. “And?”

“Imani.”

“Tea?”

“No.”

She says it before I can decide whether refusing tea makes me look more suspicious than accepting it.

Leila stands behind my chair. Miso has refused to enter the gallery, which Voss notices without asking about. I can see the cat through the window, sitting beside a planter and watching a pigeon with excessive concentration.

Voss takes a small device from his bag. It looks like a dictation recorder with a brass socket fitted into its side.

“Your mother's line. One-way for now. She won't hear you.”

I look at Leila. She nods, reluctantly.

He presses a switch.

Mum is humming. Very badly. A tune I remember because she never got past the same four notes. Something clicks. She says, “No, that won't hold,” then swears softly enough that I have to lean closer.

I cover the lower half of my face with one hand.

Leila reaches for the device. Voss lets her inspect it. She listens, checks a tiny movement in the brass socket and sets it back down.

“It's live,” she says.

Mum hums the four notes again.

“Can you tell where?” I ask.

“The lower station,” Voss says. “She made herself part of the holding circuit. I have a way to relieve her.”

Leila looks at him. “With what?”

“A willing replacement.”

“No.”

“I haven't asked {{firstName}}.”

“You don't need to.”

The woman at the front of the gallery glances over. Voss lowers his voice.

“I meant myself.”

I look at him properly for the first time.

He lets the silence sit. He is very good at leaving a space I want to fill for him.

“Why haven't you?” I ask.

“The station won't accept the person who diverted its first return line. There needs to be a witness to alter that restriction. A relative of the person holding it would be strongest.”

“Strongest,” Imani repeats.

“A connected witness.”

“You could've said that.”

Voss smiles at her without showing his teeth. “Yes.”

Leila leans forward. “You told Nadia the acknowledgement controls were manual. She trusted you.”

“They were manual.”

“You fitted a bypass.”

“To make the system stable.”

“To make it ignore refusals.”

He looks down at the loose thread on his scarf. For a moment the mild expression slips.

“People say things when they're afraid.”

The room stays quiet. On the recorder Mum says a number, corrects it and starts over. I know how she sounds when she has been working too long. She is there now.

“Eva,” I say.

Voss looks up.

“The little girl. That's who you're trying to bring back.”

His hand closes around the edge of the table.

“That's private.”

“So was Mum.”

Leila puts a hand on my shoulder. I move out from under it without thinking. Voss sees that too.

He takes a photograph from his wallet. Eva stands in the raincoat, front tooth missing, holding a small snow globe. The woman cut out of the other picture is present in this one, her arm around Eva's shoulders. She looks tired and happy and not like an explanation for anything.

“She hated snow,” he says. “Until it stopped. Then she wanted it back. Every year.”

I wait.

“I heard her through one of Nadia's early tests. Just a word. My name.”

“Did she ask you to do this?”

His eyes go flat.

“Would you have ignored your mother?”

It is the wrong question, and it still works. I look towards the recorder.

Voss pushes a card across the table. On it is an address and a time: lower entrance, six p.m. He has written the time by hand.

“You can investigate without me. I expect you will. But I have access she needs. Don't let somebody else's account of me cost you another chance.”

Outside, Miso is standing with all four paws planted against the pavement.

Voss follows my gaze. “Ask him about the collar. Ask whose name keeps it closed.”

Mum's voice stops. I reach for the recorder before I can stop myself.

Voss turns it off.

“I'll leave you to discuss it.”

He gets up, pays for four cups of tea nobody ordered, and leaves through a side door.

For a while I sit with the photograph he forgot on the table. Then Leila turns it over.

On the back, under Eva's name, is a short list of numbers. One has been crossed out and replaced. I copy all of them.

Imani notices. She says nothing until we're outside.

“You were listening,” she says.

“You sound surprised.”

“I was worried.”

It is a different answer. I keep it to myself instead of making a joke.
