# A Fare You Can Afford

You know the name Voss. It is on a plaque beside a lift in the museum, and on a Christmas card Mum used to stand behind the kettle because she didn't like the picture on the front. A silver deer, you remember. A deer with legs too long for the envelope.

Leila reads the message and turns your phone face down.

“Don't answer.”

“That's what everyone keeps saying about phones tonight.”

She takes a breath, stops, and goes to fetch another chair. There are several things she wants you to do with the chair, judging from the care with which she positions it. Sit. Stay still. Be younger.

Instead you open Mum's notebook. Behind the diagram you used is a map with three names you recognise and one you don't. The annex. A disconnected exchange room. A service building near the waterfront. The fourth name is written in a careful hand: Duat.

The cat puts a paw on the page.

“Other side,” it says. “Complicated place. Don't assume every door goes to the same part.”

“You're going to explain why you talk at some point.”

“I'm Miso. I talk because I have things to say.”

“You could say them better.”

Miso looks at Leila. “He's definitely hers.”

You aren't ready for anyone to make that observation, even a cat. You push his paw off the notebook.

Imani returns with a paper cup of hot chocolate and a packet of plain crisps. She sets the crisps beside you.

“My dad's coming here at five. He's finishing the bus first. He thinks your aunt's car broke down.” She looks at Leila. “I'd like to keep the rest of the lie small.”

Leila nods once. “You should go home with him.”

“Probably.” Imani sits on the table instead of the chair. “What was the ringing?”

Nobody answers quickly enough.

“Okay,” she says. “Still doing that.”

You turn the notebook so she can see the map. Leila's lips press together, but she lets you. You tell Imani about the double and the voice on the phone, including the part where it sounded like you. Saying it aloud in a lit room makes it sound less believable. Imani asks whether you checked the call log, then remembers she already watched you try.

Miso walks over the map.

“Nadia left a return address. We need to know whether it still receives.”

“Where?” you ask.

“The old exchange room. We take the streetcar.”

Leila begins to object. Miso looks towards the bell cupboard, where the chalk mark is already turning grey at its edges.

“If you leave that unattended,” he says, “we'll be finding out how many people in this city answer unknown numbers.”

She looks at you. You can see how badly she wants a reason to say no that you will accept. At last she takes your phone and enters a number, then presses a brass feather into your palm. It is small enough to fit between two fingers, with a hole drilled through the stem.

“Your mother's. Keep it. Call me from the exchange room, not on the way. If the screen starts showing dates it shouldn't, put it away.”

“Is Mum alive?”

“I don't know,” she says. “I am going to tell you what I know, even when it's that.”

You put the feather in the chalk box. It doesn't feel like enough to trust her with.

Outside, the cold has sharpened. Imani buys two fares from a machine while you try to work out what a cat pays. Miso climbs into the fold of your coat, apparently resolving the question.

The streetcar arrives almost empty. You check the destination display before boarding. It shows the right street, then a string of characters you can't read, then the right street again.

“We sure?” you whisper.

“Quite sure we shouldn't stand here,” Miso says from inside your collar.

Imani goes first. The driver holds up one hand.

“What did you leave behind?”

She looks at the fare reader. “We're paying.”

“Different question.”

The driver's face is hidden under the brim of his cap. His hands rest on a wheel that streetcars do not normally have. Behind him, rainwater moves upward across the windscreen.

Imani steps back until her elbow touches yours.

“The fare is an honest account,” the driver says. “Something unfinished. Something you can still name without taking it from anybody else.”

“Can we get off?” you ask.

“Yes.” He nods towards the open doors. “While they're open.”

That helps. You hadn't realised how badly you needed to hear a choice was actually yours.

You look past him. Three passengers sit under advertisements for things you have never heard of. A woman with a cardboard suitcase is picking loose threads from her gloves. A boy in a school uniform holds a loaf of bread wrapped in a tea towel. Neither looks at you.

“My mother's missing,” you say.

“That is something that happened. What did you leave?”

The doors wait. The woman stops picking her glove.

You could say the flat. The answer is technically true. You could make a joke and see whether the driver has a sense of humour, though he hasn't offered much evidence.

You look down at the chemistry worksheet still stuck to your boot by a strip of wet tape from the alley.

“I stopped putting her things back,” you say. “When my aunt tidied them away. I used to get them out again. Then it was easier not to.”

Your voice has gone quieter than you want. You hope Imani won't turn towards you.

The driver reaches for a pad of transfers. “Easier to sleep?”

“Sometimes.”

He stamps the paper and gives it to you.

“Rest isn't charged against you here.”

Miso puts his head out of your coat. “Anubis.”

The driver tips his cap. In the window beside him, his reflection has a long black muzzle and upright ears. You look at the real man, then back at the glass.

“Yes,” he says. “Please keep clear of the doors.”

You sit down with the transfer going damp in your hand. Imani sits beside you. For a whole block she says nothing.

Then she points at your boot.

“Question four's wrong.”

“I know.”

“Just checking.”

You peel the worksheet off. Outside, the streetlights stop at the edge of a wide black river.
