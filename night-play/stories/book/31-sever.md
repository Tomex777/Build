# After the Silence

For the first hour, Wren cannot bear to have anyone speak behind her. She turns at every sound, checking that it comes from a single mouth.

Imani sits where she can be seen. Julian brings water. Leila checks Nadia's burns, then Wren's hands, though there are no marks she knows how to treat.

Voss asks whether he can speak to Wren. She says no. He sits on the far side of the service room until the police come.

You tell them which buildings to check. Leila gives the statements she can make without guessing. Dennis shows them the broken bell. You keep thinking of the names in the lower station, trying to remember more than fragments.

At the hospital, Mum falls asleep halfway through asking a question. You sit beside the bed with her good hand between both of yours. When a nurse needs to check her blood pressure, you stand up too quickly and nearly fall.

“Eat something,” the nurse says.

You want to tell her that food isn't going to address the situation. Then you remember Leila has been saying similar things for eleven months, and you go to find a sandwich.

Wren is sitting by the window. She has removed the visitor sticker because the name field was blank. Julian gives her a pen. She writes WREN in large careful letters and puts the sticker back.

“Are they gone?” she asks.

“The messages?”

She nods.

“I don't know where they went.”

“You can say you don't know without looking like you're asking me to fix it.”

You sit down at the other end of the row. Outside, a hospital shuttle waits with its engine running.

“I keep trying to remember the names.”

Wren looks out of the window. “I remember some.”

“Can we write them down?”

“Later.”

“Okay.”

You sit without asking again.

When Imani's dad arrives, she goes to him at once. You watch them through the glass panel in the corridor door. She is speaking quickly, trying to explain enough. He listens with one hand on the back of her head.

Mum wakes in the afternoon. She asks for you, then for Leila, then whether everyone got out. You give her the answer Dennis got.

“All of us.”

She closes her eyes. “That's not a small thing.”

“I know.”

You do know. You also know why it doesn't feel like enough.

On your phone, the last unknown call has vanished from the log. In its place is an unsent return connection. The destination is Friday, 02:13.

You show it to Mum. She studies the screen for a long time.

“The bell's gone,” you say.

“The route was made before it broke,” she says. “One message. If you want.”

You look at her, lying in a hospital bed in Toronto. Alive. You look at the numberless call and think about what you could have said to the person you were two days ago.
