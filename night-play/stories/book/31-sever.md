# After the Silence

For the first hour, Wren cannot bear to have anyone speak behind her. She turns at every sound, checking that it comes from a single mouth.

Imani sits where she can be seen. Julian brings water. Leila checks Nadia's burns, then Wren's hands, though there are no marks she knows how to treat.

Voss asks whether he can speak to Wren. She says no. He sits on the far side of the service room until the police come.

I tell them which buildings to check. Leila gives the statements she can make without guessing. Dennis shows them the broken bell. I keep thinking of the names in the lower station, trying to remember more than fragments.

At the hospital, Mum falls asleep halfway through asking a question. I sit beside the bed with her good hand between both of mine. When a nurse needs to check her blood pressure, I stand up too quickly and nearly fall.

“Eat something,” the nurse says.

I want to tell her that food isn't going to address the situation. Then I remember Leila has been saying similar things for eleven months, and I go to find a sandwich.

Wren is sitting by the window. She has removed the visitor sticker because the name field was blank. Julian gives her a pen. She writes WREN in large careful letters and puts the sticker back.

“Are they gone?” she asks.

“The messages?”

She nods.

“I don't know where they went.”

“You can say you don't know without looking like you're asking me to fix it.”

I sit down at the other end of the row. Outside, a hospital shuttle waits with its engine running.

“I keep trying to remember the names.”

Wren looks out of the window. “I remember some.”

“Can we write them down?”

“Later.”

“Okay.”

I sit without asking again.

When Imani's dad arrives, she goes to him at once. I watch them through the glass panel in the corridor door. She is speaking quickly, trying to explain enough. He listens with one hand on the back of her head.

Mum wakes in the afternoon. She asks for me, then for Leila, then whether everyone got out. I give her the answer Dennis got.

“All of us.”

She closes her eyes. “That's not a small thing.”

“I know.”

I do know. I also know why it doesn't feel like enough.

On my phone, the last unknown call has vanished from the log. In its place is an unsent return connection. The destination is Friday, 02:13.

I show it to Mum. She studies the screen for a long time.

“The bell's gone,” I say.

“The route was made before it broke,” she says. “One message. If you want.”

I look at her, lying in a hospital bed in Toronto. Alive. I look at the numberless call and think about what I could have said to the person I was two days ago.
