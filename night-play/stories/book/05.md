# Wrong Numbers

The streetcar crosses a bridge I cannot see. Its wheels make the regular clatter of tracks, but beneath the windows there is only water and the occasional pale shape moving against the current.

Imani is still holding her fare.

“Do I need one of those transfers?” she asks.

“You'll need to tell me where you're going,” Anubis says.

“The exchange room.”

“Not an address I would recommend.”

Miso climbs from my coat onto the empty seat opposite. “The child has a return line.”

“Then the child should learn to use the return line.”

“Everyone's a teacher when they're driving.”

Anubis looks at him in the mirror. Miso lowers his ears and stops talking.

The boy holding the bread gets up at the next stop. Through the open door I see a narrow platform, a lamp and a woman standing with both hands over her mouth. The boy runs to her without looking back. By the time the doors shut, the platform has gone.

I lean forward.

“Can you take us to my mum?”

“No.”

The answer comes without hesitation. I wish he'd taken a little longer to find it.

“Don't you know where she is?”

“I know what your guide told me. I also know there are roads which turn anyone who travels them into part of the toll. I'm responsible for the passengers who use this road. That doesn't give me authority over every broken thing connected to it.”

He indicates the seats behind me. The woman with the suitcase looks away before I can meet her eyes.

I sit back. I hadn't realised I was asking him to leave other people waiting.

Miso paws at the transfer in my hand. The stamp looks like a feather enclosed by a rectangle. Under it, in ordinary English, someone has written RECEIVED WITHOUT SUBSTITUTE.

“Keep that,” he says.

“What substitute?”

“Ask the man who sent you a message.”

Imani has been watching the river. “Does everybody end up here?”

Anubis takes a long moment before answering her.

“People have imagined many roads. People have needed many roads. This is one of mine.”

At the next stop he opens the doors and points to a concrete staircase.

“Up. Keep to the rail. Don't answer anyone who asks you to hold their place.”

I want to ask what happens if I do. Miso is already out of the streetcar, his claws clicking on the concrete. Imani follows. I thank the driver, feel foolish thanking a god as if he's held a door for me, then feel more foolish for thinking the two things should cancel each other out.

“{{firstName}},” he says.

I turn.

“An address brings you to a door. It doesn't decide whether you open it.”

Before I can ask if that is advice about the call, the doors close.

At the top of the stairs I find a locked metal door. Beside it is a disconnected payphone. The receiver hangs crookedly from a cord wrapped in silver tape.

Miso jumps onto the little shelf under it. “Notebook.”

I find the return address. It isn't a building number. It is a sequence of six marks, three of which I recognise from the temporary hold.

“Say them?” I ask.

“Dial them.”

The keypad has no numbers. When I look closely I can see scratches where numbers should be, shapes that rearrange when I stop concentrating. I turn the notebook to match their orientation and press the sequence.

The call connects on the fifth mark.

“No,” Miso says quickly. “Finish it.”

A woman's voice says, “{{firstName}}?”

My finger stops above the last key.

“It's Mum. Listen. I don't have long.”

I know the voice. It has the little rough place at the bottom that arrived after she had a winter cough three years ago. I know how she says listen before telling me where she put something I should already have found.

Imani touches my wrist.

“Six marks,” she says. “There are six.”

The receiver has gone warm.

“Mum?”

Miso's claws press into the back of my hand. He isn't hurting me. He is close to it.

“Last mark.”

I press it.

The voice cuts off halfway through my name. A mechanism turns inside the door, and a young man on the other side pulls it open.

He is wearing a cardigan with one elbow held together by safety pins. His hair is flattened on one side as if he has been asleep against a book.

“You nearly sent your return line through an open channel,” he says. Then he notices Miso. “Oh, good. It's your fault.”

“Julian,” Miso says.

“Don't Julian me. I've been here since Wednesday.”

He lets me in. Beyond the door lies a room full of empty telephone booths. Their glass panels reflect a sky full of stars, although there are no windows.

One booth at the back is lit. In it, a telephone rings beneath a handwritten sign.

DO NOT PICK UP TO SEE WHO IT IS.

Underneath, someone has added: YES, THIS MEANS YOU, JULIAN.
