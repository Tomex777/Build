# Someone With Shoes On

The first landing is iced over. I discover this by sitting down on it extremely quickly.

Pain shoots up my left elbow. I manage to keep hold of the notebook. My phone slides three steps farther than I do and stops against a screw, which is the first useful screw I've encountered tonight.

The cat looks up from the next landing.

“Careful.”

I consider several responses. My aunt would not approve of any of them, though she uses the most efficient one when the hospital calls during dinner.

I get up slowly. The apartment window above me goes dark.

At street level the ladder ends eight feet above a collection of bins. I have lived in this building for four years without once thinking about the last eight feet of the fire escape. Clearly somebody on a planning committee thought fire would get bored before then.

“There's a release,” a voice says below me.

A girl in a red parka stands beside the bins, one glove raised. In her other hand she holds a hockey stick with a strip of blue tape around the blade.

“By your right foot. No, your other right.”

I find the catch. The ladder drops with a crash that sets off a car alarm halfway down the alley. The cat disappears under a skip. I climb down into a puddle deep enough to find the gap between my jeans and boots.

The girl watches me inspect the wet hem.

“You're {{firstName}}?”

I have just been warned not to open a door by someone wearing my face. This seems like a poor time to start confirming personal information.

“Who's asking?”

“Imani. Third floor. Your aunt sent me.” She takes out her phone. There is a message from Leila: “Can you check on {{firstName}}? Not answering. Don't go inside if anything seems off. Call me.”

Underneath it, Imani has written: Off how.

Leila hasn't answered.

I check my own phone. Four missed calls from my aunt. One incoming call labelled tomorrow, with no number attached. I try to show Imani, but the date changes back to today as soon as she leans closer.

“That's convenient,” I say.

“What is?”

“Nothing.”

She slips the phone into her pocket. “Right. Well, I'm on my way to my dad's shop. He can drive us to your aunt.”

“At two in the morning?”

“He's finishing a bus. I was awake. Why are you coming out of a window?”

The cat emerges from under the skip with a yoghurt lid attached to its back foot. It pauses to shake it off, fails, and turns its entire body to bite it.

“That,” I tell Imani, “is a longer answer.”

She follows my gaze. The cat freezes with the lid in its mouth.

“Is it yours?”

“No.”

“Then why does it look like you owe it money?”

I take a breath. A man with a takeaway bag passes the mouth of the alley, glances at the two of us and the ladder, and continues without slowing. The car alarm stops. Above me, one of the apartment windows opens. Somebody shouts that people have work in the morning.

I want to be that person. I would like my part in tonight's events to involve being briefly annoyed, closing a window and going back to sleep.

Imani shifts her stick to the other hand. “Your elbow's bleeding.”

I look down. A thin dark line has reached the edge of my cuff.

“It's fine.”

“I'm sure. Are you coming?”

I recognise her now. She and her dad moved into the third floor in the autumn. Leila took them food, then complained for a week that she'd given away her best container. I met Imani by the lifts and asked whether she went to my school. She said yes. I said cool. Neither of us could think of a second subject, so we watched the lift numbers together until she got out.

She knows my name because Leila uses it through the wall. I know hers because I once collected a delivery meant for her dad and kept forgetting to take it downstairs.

It occurs to me that, before tonight, this was approximately the amount of friendship I could manage without somebody asking how I was.

My phone rings.

Leila's face fills the screen, a photograph I took without warning while she was halfway through eating a peach. I answer so fast I almost drop it.

“I'm outside.”

“Outside where?”

“The building. There was someone—”

“Don't tell me your whole name. Don't tell anybody your whole name. Are you wearing the coat with the patch?”

I look at the bird.

“Yes.”

“Good. Keep it on. Imani there?”

Imani reaches for the phone. I put it on speaker instead.

“I'm here,” she says. “What's happening?”

Leila breathes out. In the background somebody calls for a porter.

“I need you both at the museum annex. The service door on the east side. Not the public museum. The little brick building across the lane. I'll send a pin.”

“Your shift?” I ask.

“I've asked somebody to cover.”

“Leila. There's someone in our flat.”

“I know.”

I stand very still. A drop of water falls from the end of the ladder onto my neck.

“How do you know?”

“We'll talk when you get here.”

“No. How—”

The call breaks into a rough crackle. I hear another voice through it, a girl's voice, very young. “Dad says don't tell them.”

Then the screen goes black.

Imani looks at me. The cat has stopped wrestling the yoghurt lid.

Behind the alley bins, the shadow cast by the streetlight lengthens until it reaches my boot. I move my foot. The shadow follows.

The cat drops the lid.

“Walk,” it says. “Don't run until you have to.”

For two seconds nobody moves.

Imani looks from me to the cat, then back again. She opens her mouth, closes it, and takes a firm grip on the hockey stick.

“Fine,” she says. “Museum. Talking later.”
