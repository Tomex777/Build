# Someone With Shoes On

The first landing is iced over. You discover this by sitting down on it extremely quickly.

Pain shoots up your left elbow. You manage to keep hold of the notebook. Your phone slides three steps farther than you do and stops against a screw, which is the first useful screw you've encountered tonight.

The cat looks up from the next landing.

“Careful.”

You consider several responses. Your aunt would not approve of any of them, though she uses the most efficient one when the hospital calls during dinner.

You get up slowly. The apartment window above you goes dark.

At street level the ladder ends eight feet above a collection of bins. You have lived in this building for four years without once thinking about the last eight feet of the fire escape. Clearly somebody on a planning committee thought fire would get bored before then.

“There's a release,” a voice says below you.

A girl in a red parka stands beside the bins, one glove raised. In her other hand she holds a hockey stick with a strip of blue tape around the blade.

“By your right foot. No, your other right.”

You find the catch. The ladder drops with a crash that sets off a car alarm halfway down the alley. The cat disappears under a skip. You climb down into a puddle deep enough to find the gap between your jeans and boots.

The girl watches you inspect the wet hem.

“You're Sam?”

You have just been warned not to open a door by someone wearing your face. This seems like a poor time to start confirming personal information.

“Who's asking?”

“Imani. Third floor. Your aunt sent me.” She takes out her phone. There is a message from Leila: Can you check on Sam? Not answering. Don't go inside if anything seems off. Call me.

Underneath it, Imani has written: Off how.

Leila hasn't answered.

You check your own phone. Four missed calls from your aunt. One incoming call labelled tomorrow, with no number attached. You try to show Imani, but the date changes back to today as soon as she leans closer.

“That's convenient,” you say.

“What is?”

“Nothing.”

She slips the phone into her pocket. “Right. Well, I'm on my way to my dad's shop. He can drive us to your aunt.”

“At two in the morning?”

“He's finishing a bus. I was awake. Why are you coming out of a window?”

The cat emerges from under the skip with a yoghurt lid attached to its back foot. It pauses to shake it off, fails, and turns its entire body to bite it.

“That,” you tell Imani, “is a longer answer.”

She follows your gaze. The cat freezes with the lid in its mouth.

“Is it yours?”

“No.”

“Then why does it look like you owe it money?”

You take a breath. A man with a takeaway bag passes the mouth of the alley, glances at the two of you and the ladder, and continues without slowing. The car alarm stops. Above you, one of the apartment windows opens. Somebody shouts that people have work in the morning.

You want to be that person. You would like your part in tonight's events to involve being briefly annoyed, closing a window and going back to sleep.

Imani shifts her stick to the other hand. “Your elbow's bleeding.”

You look down. A thin dark line has reached the edge of your cuff.

“It's fine.”

“I'm sure. Are you coming?”

You recognise her now. She and her dad moved into the third floor in the autumn. Leila took them food, then complained for a week that she'd given away her best container. You met Imani by the lifts and asked whether she went to your school. She said yes. You said cool. Neither of you could think of a second subject, so you watched the lift numbers together until she got out.

She knows your name because Leila uses it through the wall. You know hers because you once collected a delivery meant for her dad and kept forgetting to take it downstairs.

It occurs to you that, before tonight, this was approximately the amount of friendship you could manage without somebody asking how you were.

Your phone rings.

Leila's face fills the screen, a photograph you took without warning while she was halfway through eating a peach. You answer so fast you almost drop it.

“I'm outside.”

“Outside where?”

“The building. There was someone—”

“Don't tell me your whole name. Don't tell anybody your whole name. Are you wearing the coat with the patch?”

You look at the bird.

“Yes.”

“Good. Keep it on. Imani there?”

Imani reaches for the phone. You put it on speaker instead.

“I'm here,” she says. “What's happening?”

Leila breathes out. In the background somebody calls for a porter.

“I need you both at the museum annex. The service door on the east side. Not the public museum. The little brick building across the lane. I'll send a pin.”

“Your shift?” you ask.

“I've asked somebody to cover.”

“Leila. There's someone in our flat.”

“I know.”

You stand very still. A drop of water falls from the end of the ladder onto your neck.

“How do you know?”

“We'll talk when you get here.”

“No. How—”

The call breaks into a rough crackle. You hear another voice through it, a girl's voice, very young. “Dad says don't tell them.”

Then the screen goes black.

Imani looks at you. The cat has stopped wrestling the yoghurt lid.

Behind the alley bins, the shadow cast by the streetlight lengthens until it reaches your boot. You move your foot. The shadow follows.

The cat drops the lid.

“Walk,” it says. “Don't run until you have to.”

For two seconds nobody moves.

Imani looks from you to the cat, then back again. She opens her mouth, closes it, and takes a firm grip on the hockey stick.

“Fine,” she says. “Museum. Talking later.”
