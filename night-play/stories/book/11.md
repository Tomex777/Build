# The Index of Things Mislaid

Julian finds the first number from Voss's photograph in a disconnected catalogue. It refers to a room the archive no longer owns, in a building demolished before you were born.

“Helpful,” Imani says.

“The room might still be there.”

She waits for the rest of that sentence. Julian takes off his cardigan and puts it back on as if that will make the explanation easier.

“Addresses don't disappear at the same speed as buildings.”

Miso leads you through the back of a laundrette. The owner looks up from folding towels, sees Julian and says, “If you're going downstairs, bring up my trolley.”

Julian promises to try. Outside the basement door he stops you.

“I'm not promising anything else in there. I've already lost enough.”

The stairs end in a market built from shelves. High above you, light falls through a roof of catalogue cards. People move along narrow aisles with bags held close to their chests. A man is selling keys without teeth. A woman has arranged glasses of water around a notebook. Every time she turns a page, someone whispers through the glasses.

You keep your hands in your pockets.

At the centre stands a desk broad enough to eat dinner on. Behind it a man with a long, narrow face is writing in three books at once. His left hand turns a page. His right continues writing. A third pen moves without anyone holding it.

Miso sits at a respectful distance.

The man doesn't look up. “The trolley's behind the north shelves, Julian.”

“I didn't ask.”

“You were going to. And your earlier request remains under review.”

Julian's face shuts down.

You put the copied number on the desk. The third pen stops.

“A diverted address,” the man says. “Whose permission do you bring to examine it?”

“My mother's.”

“Did she give it?”

You look at the notebook. Mum wrote your name in it. She told you to find Leila. She didn't tell you to come here.

“No.”

“Then tell me whose permission you bring.”

“Mine.”

“That I can record.”

The man draws a line under your number. When he turns his head, his shadow on the shelf has a long curved beak. You remember a school project, a picture of an ibis, your mother's patient explanation that the gods had more than one form and people should stop treating the heads like a matching quiz.

“Thoth?” you ask.

“Among other ways of asking for my attention.”

“I need the address.”

“You need access to the record of its alteration. Those are different requests.”

Imani rests her hands on the edge of the desk. “What do we have to pay?”

“An account which corrects the record.”

“That's vague.”

“It becomes less vague if you stop offering me objects I have no use for.”

You hadn't offered him anything. He looks towards the notebook under your arm, and you tuck it closer before you can help yourself.

Thoth sets one of the three books in front of you. In the first column are names. In the second, brief statements. In the third, the spaces where signatures should be.

He taps a line.

NADIA MANSOUR. EXTENDED VOLUNTARY SERVICE. AUTHORITY TO DELEGATE RECEIPT.

The signature underneath resembles your mother's. It is too tidy. She never wrote the last r in Mansour properly when she was in a hurry.

“That isn't what happened,” you say.

“Then submit evidence.”

You take out the letter from Mrs. Bell's flat. The ledger copy. Mum's recording. On their own they explain pieces. Together they show who was present, who changed the route and when your mother stopped signing.

Thoth listens to the recording without expression. When Mum swears at the battery cover, the third pen makes a small mark in its book.

“You can file these,” he says. “They will be retained until the dispute is settled.”

You hold the recorder tighter. It has her voice on it. You only just got it.

Julian puts his own recorder beside yours.

“Copy,” he says. “Remember?”

You could trust the process. You could try to steal the book and run, though every aisle looks the same and the person behind the desk has been turning pages without using his hands. You could refuse the terms and try Voss's lower entrance, knowing less about what he plans.

Thoth waits. He doesn't make the decision easier by being kind.

Behind you, a trolley rolls slowly out of a side aisle and nudges Julian's ankle.

“Thank you,” Julian says stiffly.

“Ask me for something I can return,” Thoth tells him.

Julian looks down at the handle. For a moment you think he's going to argue. Instead he takes the trolley and pushes it towards the stairs.

You look back at the disputed signature. Mum's name shouldn't be sitting in a book beside a sentence she never agreed to.

You slide your evidence across the desk.
