# The Unsigned Box

By the time Imani returns, Leila has covered the kitchen table with paper. Hospital forms, archive printouts, pages copied from the notebook. She has weighed the corners down with tins because the radiator makes a draft she doesn't trust.

Julian has drawn a map on the back of a grocery receipt. It is not a reassuring map. Arrows lead into boxes labelled POSSIBLY STORAGE and DO NOT ASSUME STAIRS.

“I thought you worked there,” you say.

“I work in the part where we catalogue telephones.”

He turns the receipt over. On the other side, between onions and detergent, is a name written several times. Ruth Park.

“My sister,” he says before you ask. “I'm supposed to remember her wedding.”

“You forgot?”

“I traded a memory for access to an address book. I meant to give away a Wednesday afternoon. The person taking it said that was fine.”

“What happened?”

“I didn't specify which Wednesday. Or who I was with. Weddings aren't always on Saturdays.”

He folds the receipt so the name disappears.

Leila reaches for a pencil. “We're not bargaining with anyone.”

“Depends what they're asking,” Imani says.

Your aunt gives her the look she uses when a patient has found a treatment on the internet. Imani returns it without seeming especially concerned.

You open the ledger copy from Mrs. Bell's flat. Nadia's entry has two boxes: sender present and receiving witness. Voss's name appears in the second. There should be a signature underneath. Instead somebody has crossed the return line out.

Miso walks over the papers and sits beside it.

“That isn't a cancellation,” he says.

“What is it?”

“A diversion.”

Julian leans close. The pencil work is difficult to separate from the ruled line beneath it. He follows the marks with the blunt end of his pen.

“He sent her connection somewhere else.”

The kitchen feels too warm. You pull at the collar of your coat, which you haven't taken off since waking.

“Where?”

“The lower station. But the route's been renamed. We need its first designation.”

“Then we ask Voss.”

Leila shakes her head. “Adrian will tell you enough to make you do what he wants.”

“He's the only person who's offered to talk.”

The words land where you intend them to. You aren't proud of the small relief that follows.

Your aunt puts the pencil down. “He paid for the first equipment. He designed most of the line controls. Your mother needed a way to hold two incompatible signals apart, and he had spent half his working life solving exactly that kind of problem. At first it was useful.”

“And then his daughter died.”

Leila's eyes narrow. “How do you know that?”

You show her the photograph from the cupboard, which Julian brought in his box. The girl in the yellow coat stands beside a woman whose face has been cut out of the picture.

“Eva,” Leila says. “His wife left him after the funeral. Don't invent a story about that. I don't know why she left.”

Imani picks up one of the printouts. It is a donor report, neatly formatted, with Voss's full name and the date Nadia stopped signing the weekly test sheets.

“Eleven months,” she says. “Why is it starting now?”

Nobody has an answer ready.

Then Julian takes the bell diagram and turns it ninety degrees. The twelve scratches around its edge align with twelve smaller marks in Mum's circuit.

“They're holding intervals. Not months exactly. Load cycles.”

He draws a thirteenth mark. It crosses all the others.

“When the last interval closes, every waiting message tries to complete at the same time.”

You think of the boy with the bread. The woman waiting on the platform. The person wearing your face.

“How many?”

Julian doesn't write a number. He puts his pen down.

You take your phone out before anybody can tell you not to. Voss's message is still there. This time it displays a number. You press call.

He answers on the second ring.

“Sam. Is Leila with you?”

“Yes.”

“Good. I'd prefer she heard this too.”

That is an intelligent thing to say. You dislike him a little more for saying it.

“Where's my mother?”

“Alive. I spoke to her this morning.”

Leila holds out a hand. You don't give her the phone.

“Put her on.”

“I can't from here. I can show you the direct line. But I need you to come to the gallery on Harbord. Four o'clock. Public place. Bring whoever you need to feel comfortable.”

“Why not come here?”

“Because Miso will prevent you listening.”

The cat doesn't look away from the phone.

Voss continues. “You'll hear some very alarming things about me. Several are true. Ask yourself why nobody else has brought your mother home.”

The call ends.

Imani watches you set the phone down. “He's good at that.”

“I noticed.”

“Did you?”

You want to tell her you aren't stupid. Instead you look at the timetable on her screen, which she has been checking between sentences. Saturday morning. A rink address. A time in bold.

“You don't have to come.”

“I know.”

“You keep saying that.”

“Because you keep saying it like I don't.”

She takes the receipt map and puts it inside her phone case. It is a small practical act, and the argument you were preparing has nowhere sensible to go.
