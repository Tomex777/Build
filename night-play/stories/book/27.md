# Under the Lake

The lower entrance is inside a disused service building near the waterfront. The building is Voss's invention before it is a place: temporary walls, false measurements, a real electricity supply someone has continued paying for. From the pavement it looks like something waiting to be demolished.

Leila has brought Dennis to hold the outer line. He checks the chair she sets beside the bell case, tests its legs and sits down.

“Four minutes,” he says.

“Only after we connect the reader.”

“Then tell me when you connect the reader.”

She promises. He takes a small battery timer from his pocket. I am glad someone in the room still believes a timer can do its job without developing a second meaning.

Voss opens the inner door with a key. It leads to a staircase which should come out below ground. Instead it descends beside a wall of dark water. A fish passes on the other side, turns, and is gone.

Imani puts her hand on the rail.

“Does that stay there?”

“Usually,” Voss says.

“Try again.”

“It will hold while the outer line remains stable.”

“Better.”

We go down in single file. Miso moves ahead, stopping at every landing to sniff the corner. Wren stays near the back with Julian. She keeps looking at the water and then touching the card in her pocket.

Halfway down, the lights go out behind me.

Nobody speaks. I take out the torch and point it at the steps. The beam catches water running upward along a crack in the concrete. Something knocks against the wall on the other side, three slow impacts.

My body knows the rhythm before I do.

Imani looks back. “Not the flat.”

“I know.”

“Just making sure you know.”

The corridor at the bottom ends in a pair of stone doors. A narrow opening lies between them. Through it, I can see the lights of the lower station and, far above those lights, the upside-down shape of Toronto.

Between me and the opening stands a figure carrying a standard topped by a jackal.

He doesn't look like Anubis. Even before I can decide why, the distinction feels important. His posture is forward, alert, the way a person stands before a race begins. When he turns towards me, the end of the standard scrapes a line through the dust.

“No compelled passage,” he says.

Voss steps forward. “The operator is expecting us.”

The standard comes down between him and the doors.

“I heard the words you chose.”

Voss stops.

I find the page in Mum's notebook. The sketch is rough, but the shape of the standard is unmistakable.

Wepwawet looks at the open book.

“The conservator,” he says. “She asked better questions after the first one.”

“What was the first one?” I ask.

“How to get in without being stopped.”

“What should she have asked?”

“How to bring everyone back.”

I hear Julian draw a breath behind me. Wren moves closer to the wall. The pressure of the waiting voices has changed her shape at the edges; her sleeves look too long even for the borrowed coat.

Wepwawet examines each of us. When he reaches Miso, his eyes stop at the collar.

“That one is under claim.”

“Not by choice,” I say.

“A useful distinction. Not yet a release.”

I think of the circle on the kitchen table. I loosened the collar but didn't remove it. Voss has been quiet for too long.

“Let him go,” I tell him.

“He knows the route. If he runs—”

“Let him go.”

The stone doors shift. The gap narrows by an inch.

Voss looks at the standard, then at the cat. He says a word I don't recognise. Miso folds to the floor as the collar loosens completely.

I kneel beside him. He is breathing. His eyes stay open, fixed on Voss.

“You don't have to come farther,” I say.

He gets up slowly. Walks to the edge of the dust line. Looks through the doors.

“I know.”

He crosses back and stands beside my boot.

The opening stops narrowing.

Wepwawet looks at me. “Now ask.”

I could offer the witness receipt and negotiate passage for every person who wants it. I could invoke the office-name I learned, asking him to open a road without claiming it as mine. I could try to force the doors while they are still apart, though the standard hasn't moved an inch and the water beside me is rising.

Or I could turn back. The upper route remains open. There would still be time to cut the outer bell, but not to reach Nadia first.

I put the torch down so both hands are visible.
