# The City Above It

By late afternoon, every map on the table points towards the lake. You begin to suspect maps prefer problems which can be solved by drawing a line downwards.

Julian marks Voss's workshop, the annex and the old exchange room in different colours. Leila adds places where she has received calls since Nadia disappeared. A hospital car park. A supermarket. The bus stop outside the flat. None is on the line Julian drew.

“People,” Imani says.

He looks up.

“You're joining buildings. He connects people.”

She takes the pencil and writes names beside the points. At the hospital car park, Leila had been waiting for a colleague whose husband was dying. At the supermarket she'd met an elderly neighbour who asked after Nadia. At the bus stop, she'd been speaking to you.

The second set of lines makes an untidy knot.

Miso stands at the window. “He's getting impatient.”

The glass reflects an extra building on the opposite side of the road. It has too many windows and no ground-floor door. As you watch, a light comes on in each window, one at a time.

Your phone displays nine missed calls. It hasn't rung.

The first is from Mum. The second is from a schoolfriend you haven't spoken to properly since September. The third has no name. You don't open any of them.

Julian checks his own screen. The date has changed to Sunday.

“He's speeding the cycles.”

“Can he do that?” Imani asks.

“Apparently.”

Leila goes to the bell cupboard. She has moved it into the flat inside a locked metal case, because she finally admitted that trying to protect you from a different building was mostly protecting herself from seeing what you were doing. The chalk marks on the case are warmer than the wall around them.

“We still have time,” she says. “But less room for guessing.”

You look at the copied numbers from Voss's photograph. One is a storage reference. One is an incoming line. The crossed-out one refers to a test Nadia performed before the bypass went in.

“If we find that recording, we can show what the system was supposed to ask.”

Julian nods. “And use it to calibrate the second reader.”

The reader is in the workshop. The recording is indexed under Eva's name. For the first time, you have a plan with two tasks that can be described without saying something vague about saving everybody.

Then Dennis phones Leila. You can hear him through the receiver.

“The back room's started getting calls.”

“What back room?”

“All of them.”

Leila closes her eyes. When she opens them, she is looking at the metal case.

“I have to go back.”

You start to object. She raises one hand, then lowers it again.

“I am asking you to hear the plan before arguing. Dennis can hold the case if I show him how. I can stop the annex lines from answering automatically. Then I come to the workshop. I won't enter the other side without telling you.”

“You promise?”

“Yes.”

You check the equipment bag. Chalk, manual, notebook, ordinary torch, ordinary first-aid supplies, the feather, water. Imani adds a roll of tape and a screwdriver from her dad's tool bag. She has told him she is helping Leila move storage equipment. He told her to return the screwdriver.

Julian brings the recorder and a pair of rubber gloves. His injured hand can move at the wrist now, though the fingers remain grey.

On the street, snow has begun to fall. People hunch into their coats. At a crossing, the signal takes so long to change that a man beside you sighs and gives up waiting. He walks to the next corner instead. You watch him go, briefly jealous of a problem with another corner available.

Imani catches you looking at your phone.

“You can say you're scared without making it the whole plan.”

“I wasn't going to say it.”

“I noticed.”

You put the phone away.

At the streetcar stop, an advertisement shows a family laughing around a kitchen table. You have always disliked photographs of families laughing around kitchen tables. Nobody ever looks as if they're laughing at something specific. Now the printed mother turns her head and looks at you.

The rest of the family keep smiling in the wrong direction.

Miso presses against your boot. You move between him and the shelter glass.

“You don't have to do that,” he says.

“I know.”

The printed mother puts a hand against the inside of the advertisement. Her fingers leave marks on the glass.

Imani takes a step away. “How long until our streetcar?”

“Three minutes.”

“Let's walk.”

Nobody argues.
