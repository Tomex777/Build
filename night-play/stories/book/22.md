# A Name She Chose

Wren spends the drive watching the windscreen wipers. Each time they move she leans forward slightly, as if expecting them to take part of the view away.

Dennis notices in the mirror.

“They just shift the water.”

“I can see that.”

“Right.”

“Where does it go?”

He points to the guttering at the edge of the windscreen. She follows the movement all the way down and nods to herself.

At the annex, Leila clears a space in the storeroom. She does not put Wren inside a chalk circle. She tells everyone this before someone can suggest it.

Wren stands by the doorway, looking at the salt marks around the bell case.

“Those hurt.”

“They're for the connection,” Leila says. “We'll keep them away from you.”

“That's what he said.”

My aunt stops moving.

“Then I'll be more specific. You can sit by the radiator. Nobody will close a mark around you without asking. If we think there's a danger, we'll tell you before doing anything.”

Wren sits on the floor beside the radiator. Miso stays near the opposite wall. They watch each other with matching suspicion.

Julian puts the second reader on the table. He wants to test it immediately. I want to ask Wren about Mum. Imani goes to the vending machine and comes back with plain crackers because nobody knows whether Wren can eat.

She can hold one. She turns it over several times.

“What happens?”

“You bite it,” Imani says.

“After.”

“I don't usually plan that far with a cracker.”

Wren looks at her. Then she smiles, briefly and uncertainly. It is the first expression I've seen on her face that doesn't seem borrowed.

When she bites the cracker, half of it falls into her lap. She stares at the crumbs, annoyed.

I sit on the floor a little way from her.

“My mother,” I begin.

“I know.”

“Did you hurt her?”

Wren puts the remaining cracker down.

“I made the room move.”

“Why?”

“Because she was leaving.”

I have to breathe before asking the next question.

“Did you know what would happen to Julian?”

She looks at his bandaged hand. “I know now.”

Julian doesn't say it's all right. He sets down the wire he is holding and turns towards her.

“You can't do that again.”

“I don't want him to put me back.”

“That's a different answer.”

“Yes,” she says. “I know.”

The room is quiet enough for me to hear the vending machine compressor start in the corridor.

I ask what happens when the Exchange opens fully. Wren rubs a crumb between her fingers until it disappears.

“All the voices come through. Dad says we can find the right one.”

“Voss,” I say, then regret correcting her so sharply.

She looks down.

“Voss says.”

“What do you hear?”

She presses her hands against the sides of her head. “A lot. Some are small. Some are shouting. Some don't know they're finished.”

“Can you stop them?”

“Sometimes. If I use a face they like. Then they listen.”

I think of my own face outside the flat. The note under the glass.

“Why mine?”

“Your mother said it when she was tired. Your name. I heard it so much I thought it was somewhere.”

“What do you mean?”

“A place she was trying to go.”

I look away. Imani moves the cracker packet a little closer to Wren, although she hasn't asked for another.

Julian asks about the changing load. Wren is better at explaining that than at explaining herself. The messages have been accumulating because Voss keeps the receiving lines open. Every unanswered call borrows capacity from the next. Nadia has been holding the group apart with one manual control. By dawn, there will be too many connections for her to distinguish.

“The second reader can ask each one whether it wants to go,” Julian says.

“It can ask,” Wren says. “It doesn't know who's answering.”

“Can you tell?” I ask.

She watches me for a long time.

“Sometimes.”

“If you help—”

“No.”

I stop.

She picks the cracker up again. “You ask and then you do the other thing. You say if, and then I have to.”

Leila sits down beside the table. She does it slowly, with her hands visible on her knees.

“We need to work out what happens to you if the messages leave,” she says. “We can't pretend we already know.”

Wren looks at her. The radiator clicks behind her shoulder.

“I don't want to disappear.”

I cannot promise she won't. The temptation is so strong it makes my mouth feel full of words I shouldn't use.

Instead I ask, “Can we find out before we decide?”

Wren draws one knee up. The glove on her left hand has begun to slip through her fingers. She pushes it back into place.

“Maybe.”

I sit with her while Julian begins testing the reader. For a while nobody asks Wren to do anything useful.
