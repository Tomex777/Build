# The Person in the Group Chat

My friend Ella messages while Julian is sorting the gallery numbers. She sends a photograph of the chemistry board, followed by a second photograph because the first is blurred.

I haven't replied to her last four messages. I meant to. At first I didn't know what to say. Then replying required explaining why I hadn't replied, and the explanation became another message I couldn't finish.

Now she writes: “Your aunt said medical. You okay?”

I type yes, delete it, and type: At home. Family thing. Thanks for the notes.

Three dots appear.

“You don't have to do a whole update. Just send a full stop sometimes so I know you're not dead.”

I stare at the screen. The word dead looks different when someone uses it carelessly. It also looks like a word a friend might use because she has no idea how to ask the careful question without sounding like everybody else.

I send a full stop.

Her reply comes immediately: Incredible. Communication.

I laugh before remembering I am supposed to be frightened. It is a small sound, but Julian looks up.

“Something useful?”

“Yes,” I say. “Not about this.”

I take the phone into the sitting room and call her. She answers with, “Oh. A voice. Didn't know yours still worked.”

“Sometimes.”

She tells me about school. Someone has moved my locker contents because the hinge broke again. My chemistry teacher has decided to give everyone an extra week on the project, which Ella calls a personal apology from the universe. A boy I don't particularly like has been saying things about my attendance. She tells me she dealt with it, then refuses to specify how.

For several minutes I am allowed to be irritated by the correct small things. I sit on the floor beside the sofa and pick at a loose thread while she complains about the cafeteria's attempt at soup.

Then she says, “Someone rang my mum last night. Asked for you.”

My hand stops.

“Who?”

“Didn't say. Mum thought it was school. At three in the morning, so obviously not school, but you know how people sound official and she starts finding a pen.”

“What did they ask?”

“Your full name. Whether she knew where you were.”

I get up.

“Ella. Did she tell them?”

There is a pause. “I don't think so. Why?”

I go to the kitchen door and beckon Julian. He sees my face and puts his papers down.

“I need you to ask her. Don't call the number back.”

“{{firstName}}?”

“I know it sounds strange. Please don't call it back.”

Ella goes quiet in a way that is worse than a question. I hear her walk into another room and speak to her mother. The answer comes through indistinctly. Then Ella returns.

“She said she didn't know. She told them to call your aunt.”

I close my eyes.

“Okay. That's good.”

“Why?”

Julian holds out a scrap of paper. On it he has written: If they have a voice, they try its connections.

I think of my unanswered messages. The group chat. A person asking after I because she wants me to show up in class, accidentally becoming a line the Exchange can use.

“I can't explain all of it yet,” I say. “Something happened with Mum's work. Somebody is trying to get information through people who know us.”

That is true enough to sound worse than the version involving a talking cat.

Ella says, “Do I tell somebody?”

“Your mum. My aunt. Yes.”

Leila has come in behind Julian. I pass her the phone. She gives Ella a number for Dennis, then says the calls are part of an ongoing safety problem and should not be answered. She doesn't tell Ella she imagined anything. I hear the care in that choice.

Afterwards, Ella asks for me back.

“You could've told me there was a problem.”

“I didn't know how.”

“I didn't ask for a perfect explanation.”

I sit down again. The loose thread has wrapped around one finger so tightly it has left a red line.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

I unwind the thread.

“I think I'm starting to.”

She tells me she will keep the notes. If I need to miss another week, say so. If I don't want people asking questions, say that too. She doesn't promise to make everyone behave. She does promise to send a full stop at lunch.

I agree to send one back.

When the call ends, Julian adds Ella's household to the map. He writes only the information needed to block the diverted request, then puts the rest of the page facedown.

I recognise the distinction from Mrs. Bell's letter.

“I don't want everyone I know pulled into this.”

“Then we give the line fewer ways to mistake concern for permission.”

He shows me how to mark a receiving refusal on an ordinary piece of paper, tied to a number rather than a person. Leila calls Ella's mother and talks her through it as an unusual precaution. I can hear how odd the instructions sound. I can also hear the other woman deciding to do them anyway.

For the next twenty minutes I copy the mark onto cards for the households Leila knows have received calls. It is repetitive work. My hand gets tired. Nothing dramatic happens.

When one of the lines on Julian's map goes dark, I keep working.
