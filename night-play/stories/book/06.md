# Seven Minutes

Julian puts the kettle on, checks the water level, and takes it off again.

“Never mind. It might be river water.”

I follow his gaze to a stain on the ceiling. It has the shape of the building above me, though the building changes while I watch. A chimney disappears. A fire escape grows down through one edge. My own kitchen window opens briefly in the middle.

“Is that how you saw the flat?” I ask Miso.

He jumps onto a desk and settles his tail over his paws. “One way.”

Julian makes a sound that suggests there are several worse ways.

He is nineteen, he tells me, studying part-time and working for the small independent archive attached to the annex. He says independent with a care that suggests he has already had to explain it to a tax office. Most of the collection is ordinary: old operator manuals, repair logs, donated equipment. The part that isn't ordinary has been making his working week increasingly difficult.

“My supervisor used to deal with that bit.”

“Nadia?”

He nods.

“You knew my mum.”

“I still know her.”

For that sentence alone, I could forgive him a lot. He seems to realise he has offered something larger than he meant to, because he begins moving papers into neat piles.

The lit booth rings again.

Imani points to it. “What happens if somebody picks up?”

“Usually someone asks for somebody who isn't there. If you tell them they're wrong, they try the next place. If you offer to help, the system decides you're available.”

“Available for what?”

Julian looks at Miso.

The cat answers. “To carry the message.”

He says it as if that should explain everything. I wait until he notices I haven't understood.

“The original line let a caller send a message without making the receiver answer. Whoever built this version removed that safeguard.”

Julian takes a recorder from a drawer. It is the same model as the one in the annex. A strip of tape on its back says NADIA: BACKUP.

My mother speaks when he presses play.

“Test forty-three. The holding mark remains temporary. Seven minutes if the line is stable. Less if it isn't. We need a second reader at the receiving end. Adrian says we can automate the acknowledgement. I disagree.”

A pause. A paper being turned. In the background I hear myself, younger, complaining about homework. Julian stops the recorder when he sees my face.

“Sorry.”

“Play it.”

“There's quite a lot.”

“Play it.”

Mum's voice returns. She describes a circuit she has been testing, then corrects herself, then starts the explanation again without recording over the first attempt. I have spent months trying to preserve every memory I have of her. Here she is getting annoyed at a loose battery cover.

I need to sit down. I do it before anybody asks.

The recording ends with her saying, “{{firstName}}, don't put that near the—” and a loud clatter.

I remember the day. I dropped a screwdriver into a ceramic mug she'd been using to hold broken components. I thought she was angry because it was her favourite mug. Now, listening to the quick movement afterwards, I wonder what else was inside it.

Julian slides the recorder across the desk.

“Keep it. It's a copy.”

“Why haven't you given this to Leila?”

His mouth tightens.

“Because I couldn't get back through the door until someone dialled the full return address. I did tell you about Wednesday.”

Miso examines a paw.

I take the chalk from its box. On the desk I draw the holding mark, exactly as I drew it in the cupboard. Julian sets an old desk fan beside it and plugs the fan into a socket. Nothing happens.

“Magic doesn't work because chalk is expensive,” he says. “That shape holds a relationship. You need to tell it what relationship.”

I point to the notebook. “It doesn't say that.”

“It does. In the little note you skipped.”

It does. I skipped the note because it looked like a caption.

He puts my hand on the fan's plug, then makes me describe the connection between the socket and the motor. I say electricity. He asks what electricity is doing. I say making the fan spin. He asks what I want to stop.

“The fan spinning.”

“Then you want the mechanism, not the connection.”

Imani, who has been crouching beside the fan, turns its power switch on. It begins to rattle.

“Or that,” Julian says.

I let go of the plug. I want to laugh, but the room has started to feel unfair. My mother did something here that kept her away for eleven months. There should be a more difficult explanation than a switch I didn't notice.

Julian tears a sheet of paper in half and writes YOUR END on one piece, MY END on the other. He gives one to me.

“Try it on something that needs two people.”

I put the paper on the desk. Imani holds the second half. When I close the final line of the chalk mark, the two pieces rise an inch from the wood and stay there.

My fingers ache. I concentrate on the torn edges, on where they would fit together. The paper begins to shake.

“Breathe,” Imani says.

I breathe. The pieces settle.

Julian starts a timer. I hold the mark for forty-one seconds before my hand cramps and the papers fall.

It feels like failure until he writes forty-one in the margin beside my mother's first test.

Her time was twelve seconds.

At the back of the room, the ringing phone stops. A printer I hadn't noticed begins feeding out paper. Julian reaches it before the sheet can fall.

Three lines are printed across the top.

RECEIVING POINT CONFIRMED.

NADIA {{surname|upper}}: CONNECTION ACTIVE.

COLLECTION SCHEDULE: SUNDAY, FIRST LIGHT.

It is Friday morning. I have two days.
