# The Operator

You ask Julian to describe the choice one more time.

He looks at Mum. At Leila. At Imani, who has gone very still.

“One receiver,” he says. “No forced continuation. We disconnect every other claim. The station becomes a place you operate, not a place that owns everyone attached to it.”

“Can you reach it from outside?”

“Yes. With the second reader.”

“Can you get me out?”

He doesn't look away.

“I don't know.”

Mum says your name. You turn towards her. She is trying to let go of the cable, trying to make the other choice possible before you finish considering this one.

“Don't do it because you're my child,” she says.

You kneel beside the panel. You could still break the bell. That choice remains. The thought helps you keep the next words steady.

“I'm not taking the old instruction.”

Julian lowers the lever enough to open a blank receiving line.

You take the brass feather from the box. Mum's fingers close around your wrist.

For a moment you stay like that, neither pulling away nor giving in. Her hand is warm above the burned cloth. You want another hour in the kitchen. You want her to complain about your hair again.

“I need you to keep looking,” you tell her.

She closes her eyes. When she opens them, there is no agreement in her face. There is understanding, which is different and harder to accept.

“I will.”

You write your name beside the new receiving slot. Then you say what you accept, as carefully as Leila taught you to define a connection. Only the waiting messages. No authority over their senders. No new compulsory calls. A line that can be reached from outside without placing the caller under claim.

The feather warms in your hand.

Julian records each condition. Wren repeats it, listening for a response the rest of you cannot distinguish. Imani checks the page before letting you move to the next line.

The indicator shows your name.

You close the holding mark around yourself.

The load arrives slowly enough for you to know you could still stop before the second stage. You say that aloud. Julian confirms it. You take one more breath.

“Continue.”

Mum's hand comes free.

She folds to the floor. Leila catches her. You want to go to them, but the room has changed its sense of distance. The table is near and also impossibly far away. You can hear a woman asking for her son. A boy wants to know whether his bread is still warm. An old man is angry about a set of tools.

You say wait, and the voices wait. Not forever. Long enough for you to understand the difference.

Julian disconnects the old receiving loop. Wren takes her card from the table. Her face steadies as the borrowed voices move towards you instead.

Imani comes close enough that you can see the worn tape on the spare stick she brought from home.

“You don't get to turn into a job,” she says.

“I'll try not to.”

“No. Pick something ordinary. Something we can ask you about.”

You look around the room. There is a chair near the switchboard, its seat split along one seam.

“The chair's terrible.”

She nods, furious and crying now. “We'll start with that.”

Miso stays beside your chalk mark. Leila tells him to come. He looks at her and lies down.

“Miso,” you say.

He puts his head on his paws. “I'm not under claim.”

You cannot make him leave without contradicting the whole connection you just made.

Mum reaches you one last time. You are not sure whether she crossed the room or the room allowed her. She puts her uninjured hand against your cheek.

“I am going to be very difficult about this,” she says.

You laugh. It hurts. “I know.”

Then Leila takes her towards the stairs. Julian carries the reader. Wren stops in the doorway and raises her hand. Imani follows last, looking back until the angle of the corridor takes you out of sight.

The station settles. It is smaller now. No borrowed corridors. No locked inner room. Just the table, the switchboard, the chair and a cat who has chosen to remain.

You sit down. The split seam catches your coat.

At the first socket, a light comes on.

You check the receiving conditions before lifting the handset.

“Hello,” you say. “You can leave a message. You don't have to stay.”
