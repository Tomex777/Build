# The Little Workers

Julian finds the box while searching for a spare socket. It sits behind two old lamps in a cupboard your mother told you contained nothing interesting.

Inside are six little clay figures, each about the length of your thumb. They have rough bodies, plain faces and a mark on the back. You take one out. It is surprisingly heavy.

“Shabti?” you ask.

“Modern practice models,” Julian says. “Nadia made them. Don't start thinking the museum lends out ancient funerary equipment.”

There are notes under the tissue. Mum has drawn a picture of a worker figure holding a basket, then written several pages about why telling a created thing to perform an endless task is a poor starting point for any safe experiment.

Her own models are supposed to carry a defined load from one point to another and stop when the task is complete.

Imani turns a figure over.

“So little delivery people.”

“Little load markers,” Julian says.

“That's less fun.”

You line them up on the table. The figures do not look particularly interested in either description.

Mum's notes describe a way to divide a connection among several temporary holders. It is not enough for the lower station's load. It might be enough to steady a reader while somebody changes a setting.

Julian gets the second practice handset. You draw the required marks. Imani positions the little figures along the cable, using a ruler because she has decided guessing is how everybody got into trouble.

The first figure moves when you complete the circuit.

Not much. It lifts one foot, puts it down and tips over.

Imani catches it.

“Strong start.”

Julian takes it from her and checks the mark. “The subject's wrong. You're asking it to hold the entire call, not one defined segment.”

You alter the line. This time the figure stands with both feet on the table. The second follows it. Then the third.

For a few seconds, the handset cable hangs in a precise curve between them without touching the wood.

You grin before you can help it.

Then the fourth figure turns towards you.

“Further instruction?” it asks in your own voice.

You stop grinning.

Julian reaches for the circuit. Imani puts one hand above it without touching.

“Ask it to stop first.”

You do. The figure remains standing.

“Further instruction?”

The fifth figure turns. Then the sixth.

Your drawing has a closed return at the far end. You copied it from an early page in Mum's notes, before the correction on the next sheet. The figures are not ignoring you. The circuit has no recognised completion.

They begin passing the little load back to the first one.

The cable tightens around the ruler. Imani moves her hand away. A crack appears in one clay shoulder.

You want to snatch the figure up. Julian catches your wrist.

“Connection,” he says. “Not the body.”

You take the paper Leila taught you from your pocket. The release sequence is simple enough to remember until you need it. You set it beside the practice mark and copy the first outer line.

The little figures turn with you, all six faces following the chalk.

“Further instruction?”

“End this task,” you say. “No replacement task. No further holding.”

The outer mark opens.

One figure sits down. Then another. The cable falls onto the table. The last figure says your name once, quietly, and becomes clay again.

Nobody speaks for a while.

You pick up the one with the cracked shoulder. Mum's thumbprint is visible near its base. You rub a finger over it and put the figure back in the box.

Julian writes the corrected sequence in large letters. Imani puts a strip of blue tape on the page you missed.

Leila comes in from the sitting room and looks at the table.

“What did you learn?”

You could say the figures work. It would be true. You could also omit the part where they nearly broke while following exactly what you asked.

Instead you show her both pages.

She studies them, then the cracked clay.

“Nadia should have marked this better.”

You are ready to defend Mum. Then you remember you were the one standing here when the cable tightened.

“Yes,” you say.

Leila sits down and helps you label every earlier version. She doesn't lecture. She makes you check each page against the next. It takes another half-hour.

At the end, you pack three unbroken figures in a padded tin. They aren't a solution to the whole station. They are a tool for a defined job. You write the exact job on a card and put it in the tin with them.

Imani reads it before you close the lid.

“Good,” she says. “They get to go home when they're done.”
