# The Little Workers

Julian finds the box while searching for a spare socket. It sits behind two old lamps in a cupboard my mother told me contained nothing interesting.

Inside are six little clay figures, each about the length of my thumb. They have rough bodies, plain faces and a mark on the back. I take one out. It is surprisingly heavy.

“Shabti?” I ask.

“Modern practice models,” Julian says. “Nadia made them. Don't start thinking the museum lends out ancient funerary equipment.”

There are notes under the tissue. Mum has drawn a picture of a worker figure holding a basket, then written several pages about why telling a created thing to perform an endless task is a poor starting point for any safe experiment.

Her own models are supposed to carry a defined load from one point to another and stop when the task is complete.

Imani turns a figure over.

“So little delivery people.”

“Little load markers,” Julian says.

“That's less fun.”

I line them up on the table. The figures do not look particularly interested in either description.

Mum's notes describe a way to divide a connection among several temporary holders. It is not enough for the lower station's load. It might be enough to steady a reader while somebody changes a setting.

Julian gets the second practice handset. I draw the required marks. Imani positions the little figures along the cable, using a ruler because she has decided guessing is how everybody got into trouble.

The first figure moves when I complete the circuit.

Not much. It lifts one foot, puts it down and tips over.

Imani catches it.

“Strong start.”

Julian takes it from her and checks the mark. “The subject's wrong. You're asking it to hold the entire call, not one defined segment.”

I alter the line. This time the figure stands with both feet on the table. The second follows it. Then the third.

For a few seconds, the handset cable hangs in a precise curve between them without touching the wood.

I grin before I can help it.

Then the fourth figure turns towards me.

“Further instruction?” it asks in my own voice.

I stop grinning.

Julian reaches for the circuit. Imani puts one hand above it without touching.

“Ask it to stop first.”

I do. The figure remains standing.

“Further instruction?”

The fifth figure turns. Then the sixth.

My drawing has a closed return at the far end. I copied it from an early page in Mum's notes, before the correction on the next sheet. The figures are not ignoring me. The circuit has no recognised completion.

They begin passing the little load back to the first one.

The cable tightens around the ruler. Imani moves her hand away. A crack appears in one clay shoulder.

I want to snatch the figure up. Julian catches my wrist.

“Connection,” he says. “Not the body.”

I take the paper Leila taught me from my pocket. The release sequence is simple enough to remember until I need it. I set it beside the practice mark and copy the first outer line.

The little figures turn with me, all six faces following the chalk.

“Further instruction?”

“End this task,” I say. “No replacement task. No further holding.”

The outer mark opens.

One figure sits down. Then another. The cable falls onto the table. The last figure says my name once, quietly, and becomes clay again.

Nobody speaks for a while.

I pick up the one with the cracked shoulder. Mum's thumbprint is visible near its base. I rub a finger over it and put the figure back in the box.

Julian writes the corrected sequence in large letters. Imani puts a strip of blue tape on the page I missed.

Leila comes in from the sitting room and looks at the table.

“What did you learn?”

I could say the figures work. It would be true. I could also omit the part where they nearly broke while following exactly what I asked.

Instead I show her both pages.

She studies them, then the cracked clay.

“Nadia should have marked this better.”

I am ready to defend Mum. Then I remember I was the one standing here when the cable tightened.

“Yes,” I say.

Leila sits down and helps me label every earlier version. She doesn't lecture. She makes me check each page against the next. It takes another half-hour.

At the end, I pack three unbroken figures in a padded tin. They aren't a solution to the whole station. They are a tool for a defined job. I write the exact job on a card and put it in the tin with them.

Imani reads it before I close the lid.

“Good,” she says. “They get to go home when they're done.”
