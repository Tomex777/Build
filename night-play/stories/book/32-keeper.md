# A Way to Call Home

You check the return line against Mum's manual before using it. One message, back to the point where the first claim began. The date is a reserved receiving slot, a place in the route rather than a promise about which day the speaker belongs to.

It explains the wrong date on your phone. It does not make hearing your own breathing less strange.

“Whatever happens,” you say, “do not open the door.”

You give yourself the notebook. The kitchen drawer. You start one last sentence and the line closes before you know how to finish it.

Miso lifts his head from the blanket.

“Done?”

“I think so.”

“Then somebody's waiting.”

You turn to the next light.

The first week is hard in ways you didn't rehearse. You miss being able to leave a room because you are tired of it. You miss food you can complain about. You miss the sound the flat's radiator makes before it warms up.

You do not miss school until Julian brings your chemistry work through the controlled line. Then you miss the whole building, including the corridor where the lockers never close properly.

Mum calls every evening. Sometimes she talks about the receiving point she is trying to build. Sometimes she tells you what she bought at the supermarket. When she begins explaining an experiment without asking how you are, you interrupt her. The first time, she is hurt. The second time, she catches herself.

Leila keeps the calls from becoming only research. She asks about sleep, then listens when you say you don't know whether you are sleeping or merely resting between messages. She writes it down without pretending the answer is reassuring.

Imani brings a chair. Getting it through the controlled route takes most of a Saturday and an argument with a door which appears not to recognise furniture as an authorised visitor. She refuses to leave it behind. When it finally arrives, the seat is scratched but intact.

“Sit,” she says.

You sit. It is an excellent chair.

She stands beside you for a while, looking at the board. A light comes on, then another. You let them wait. That is permitted now.

“I got the place,” she says.

“At the selection?”

She nods.

You get up so quickly the chair scrapes backwards. She laughs, then you are hugging her, and for a minute the station contains a celebration which has nothing to do with being brave.

Wren visits with Julian. She is learning to write messages which belong only to her. Most are questions. Some are complaints about how many ordinary words have more than one meaning. She brings Miso a knitted mouse. He ignores it until she leaves, then carries it to the blanket.

Voss has not tried to call. The investigation continues above. Nadia has supplied the original manuals and signed a statement about her own work. Julian tells you the records will be complicated. You believe him. You have developed a strong dislike of stories which end when the person responsible looks sad enough.

You help a few callers each day. You turn down requests that would make somebody else answer by force. You ask the right question before accepting a message. Sometimes a receiver refuses. The refusal is recorded, and the line closes. No one is punished for it.

At the end of the third week, Mum reaches the station through the visitor route. Not a voice. A person. She has a bag of sandwiches and several pages of calculations held together by a bulldog clip.

She puts the calculations down first.

“How are you?” she asks.

You look at her hand. It is healing. You look at the sandwiches, the ordinary grease showing through the paper.

“Angry,” you say. “Sometimes. Homesick.”

She sits in the old chair, which Imani repaired rather than throwing away.

“Tell me.”

So you do. Not everything. Enough to begin.

Later, she shows you the calculations. There may be a way to divide the station's role among several consenting operators, each with a defined shift and a way out. It is not ready. It is not a promise she can safely make. She tells you both facts before asking whether you want to help test it.

You read the first page. Make a correction in the margin. Ask a question.

Mum smiles at the familiar impatience in your voice.

On the switchboard, a light comes on. You check its conditions, then lift the receiver.

“Hello,” you say.

Miso closes his eyes in the warm patch beside your new chair. Mum opens her notebook. Somewhere above you, people are having an ordinary evening, and the controlled line home remains open.

THE END
