# The Person Outside

I had checked the hallway before turning the lock. Mrs. Rinaldi's shopping trolley stood beside the lift, and somebody had left a wet glove on top of the recycling box. Nobody else was there. I remember this with the unfair clarity that arrives when I need a different explanation.

Now the phone is ringing in my hand, showing tomorrow's date.

It is Friday. I check the little date above the microwave, which has said January 1 since a power cut two winters ago. Useless. The laptop on the kitchen table says Friday. The chemistry worksheet underneath it says my name, {{fullName}}, and beside the first question I've written a number I already know is wrong.

Outside, someone knocks three times.

My aunt has a key. I tell myself she could have lost it. Leila loses pens, hair ties, the small scissors she buys to replace the small scissors she loses. She has never lost her keys. They hang from a bright orange strap marked EMERGENCY, which she chose because, in her words, nobody steals something that looks like a responsibility.

The sound comes again. Patient. I put the phone down on the counter without ending the call.

“Leila?”

“Open up, {{firstName}}.”

My own voice. Not a recording played back through a speaker. A person with my voice, out in the hallway, standing approximately where Mrs. Rinaldi parks her trolley.

I step away from the door. One of my socks catches a splinter in the laminate and makes a small, ordinary ripping sound.

Leila has spent eleven months making lists for me. Frightened? Call her. Can't sleep? Try that boring history podcast. Someone outside? Use the peephole, don't start an argument through the door. I have a plan for every emergency she can name, and no plan at all for this one.

Through the peephole, a person in my winter coat is holding a phone to their ear.

I know it is my coat because of the triangular patch near the pocket, where Mum fixed a tear with a scrap from an old curtain. A grey curtain with yellow birds. I had complained that it looked like something for a five-year-old, then refused to let her replace it.

The person raises their eyes to the peephole.

For a moment my brain does something embarrassing. It tries to make this a mirror. It adjusts the height, the angle, the shape of the light. Then the person smiles while my mouth stays open.

I cover the peephole with my palm.

On the kitchen counter, my phone says, “{{firstName}}?”

I don't remember deciding to pick it up.

“I'm here.”

The breath at the other end catches. Behind it, water runs over something hollow. A bell rings once, far away.

“Whatever happens,” my voice says, “do not open the door.”

I look at the door. The lock is turning slowly. I can see the little brass tab moving, fraction by fraction, although there is no key on my side.

“How do I stop it?”

“Blue notebook. Kitchen drawer.”

“What?”

“Mum's notebook. Get it. And {{firstName}}—”

The line goes dead.

For eleven months, adults have paused before saying my mother's name. My teachers do it. My dentist once did it with two fingers in my mouth, which made me briefly, furiously glad she hadn't gone into medicine. They say missing, and then wait to find out what version of that word I'm living with today.

The voice on the phone said Mum as if she could still come into the kitchen and tell me to stop picking the bird patch off my coat.

I open the drawer under the kettle.

Takeaway menus. A packet of birthday candles. Elastic bands Leila insists are useful, although their main function is to attach themselves to everything else. Under a bent photograph of me at a science fair lies a blue cloth notebook.

I've searched this drawer before. Twice. Once for a tin opener, once because I thought Mum might have left something my aunt hadn't found.

The notebook is warm.

I turn back its cover. Nadia {{surname}}, it says. If found, please return. Then her email address, the one I still sometimes write messages to and leave in drafts.

Beneath that, in a different pen: “{{firstName}}, if you're reading this, find Leila before you try anything clever.”

My knees go unreliable. I sit down on the edge of a chair and read the line twice. It is recognisably her handwriting, including the dot she always puts too far to the right of an i. I press my thumb against it, hard enough to crease the page.

The lock clicks.

I push the chair under the door handle. It fits badly. Leila has told the landlord six times that the kitchen chairs are too wobbly to use safely. Tonight I would like them to be considerably less affordable.

“{{firstName}},” the person outside says. “You left me out here.”

“You're wearing my coat.”

“So are you.”

I look down. The coat is over the chair at the table, exactly where I left it after school.

A little black paw appears outside the kitchen window and taps the glass.

I turn so quickly the notebook falls shut. On the fire escape sits a cat, fur puffed out against the cold. It has one pale whisker, a nick in an ear and the offended expression of an animal who has been waiting a long time.

It taps again.

I can hear the chair scraping across the floor behind me.

The window has been painted shut at the bottom. I hook my fingers under the frame and pull. Nothing. The cat mouths something through the glass. I stare at it.

It rolls its eyes, leans closer and says, distinctly, “Latch.”

I undo the latch.

The frame shoots upward. Cold air stings the wet inside of my nose. The cat climbs over the sill, shakes snow onto the chemistry worksheet, and pushes past me towards the notebook.

“Coat,” it says. “Phone. Shoes. Leave the laptop.”

I haven't mentioned the laptop.

“The expensive square,” the cat adds, impatiently. “Humans always go back for the expensive square.”

A screw falls from the door handle and bounces under the fridge.

I put on my coat. The real one, with the patch that catches on my thumb. I shove my feet into my boots, grab the notebook and phone, and climb through the window.

At the top of the fire escape, I look back.

My door opens three inches against the chair. In the gap I see my own hand, then an eye. The person inside my apartment looks towards the window. Its mouth moves. I can't hear the words over the traffic below.

The cat bites my sleeve and pulls.

I follow it down.
