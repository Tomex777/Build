# The Person Outside

You had checked the hallway before turning the lock. Mrs. Rinaldi's shopping trolley stood beside the lift, and somebody had left a wet glove on top of the recycling box. Nobody else was there. You remember this with the unfair clarity that arrives when you need a different explanation.

Now the phone is ringing in your hand, showing tomorrow's date.

It is Friday. You check the little date above the microwave, which has said January 1 since a power cut two winters ago. Useless. The laptop on the kitchen table says Friday. The chemistry worksheet underneath it says your name, Sam Mansour, and beside the first question you've written a number you already know is wrong.

Outside, someone knocks three times.

Your aunt has a key. You tell yourself she could have lost it. Leila loses pens, hair ties, the small scissors she buys to replace the small scissors she loses. She has never lost her keys. They hang from a bright orange strap marked EMERGENCY, which she chose because, in her words, nobody steals something that looks like a responsibility.

The sound comes again. Patient. You put the phone down on the counter without ending the call.

“Leila?”

“Open up, Sam.”

Your own voice. Not a recording played back through a speaker. A person with your voice, out in the hallway, standing approximately where Mrs. Rinaldi parks her trolley.

You step away from the door. One of your socks catches a splinter in the laminate and makes a small, ordinary ripping sound.

There are things you can do. Your aunt has spent eleven months providing lists of them. If you're frightened, call me. If you can't sleep, put on that boring history podcast. If you hear something outside, use the peephole, don't start an argument through the door. A plan for every emergency she can name.

Through the peephole, a person in your winter coat is holding a phone to their ear.

You know it is your coat because of the triangular patch near the pocket, where Mum fixed a tear with a scrap from an old curtain. A grey curtain with yellow birds. You had complained that it looked like something for a five-year-old, then refused to let her replace it.

The person raises their eyes to the peephole.

For a moment your brain does something embarrassing. It tries to make this a mirror. It adjusts the height, the angle, the shape of the light. Then the person smiles while your mouth stays open.

You cover the peephole with your palm.

On the kitchen counter, your phone says, “Sam?”

You don't remember deciding to pick it up.

“I'm here.”

The breath at the other end catches. Behind it, water runs over something hollow. A bell rings once, far away.

“Whatever happens,” your voice says, “do not open the door.”

You look at the door. The lock is turning slowly. You can see the little brass tab moving, fraction by fraction, although there is no key on your side.

“How do I stop it?”

“Blue notebook. Kitchen drawer.”

“What?”

“Mum's notebook. Get it. And Sam—”

The line goes dead.

For eleven months, adults have paused before saying your mother's name. Your teachers do it. Your dentist once did it with two fingers in your mouth, which made you briefly, furiously glad she hadn't gone into medicine. They say missing, and then wait to find out what version of that word you're living with today.

The voice on the phone said Mum as if she could still come into the kitchen and tell you to stop picking the bird patch off your coat.

You open the drawer under the kettle.

Takeaway menus. A packet of birthday candles. Elastic bands Leila insists are useful, although their main function is to attach themselves to everything else. Under a bent photograph of you at a science fair lies a blue cloth notebook.

You've searched this drawer before. Twice. Once for a tin opener, once because you thought Mum might have left something your aunt hadn't found.

The notebook is warm.

You turn back its cover. Nadia Mansour, it says. If found, please return. Then her email address, the one you still sometimes write messages to and leave in drafts.

Beneath that, in a different pen: Sam, if you're reading this, find Leila before you try anything clever.

Your knees go unreliable. You sit down on the edge of a chair and read the line twice. It is recognisably her handwriting, including the dot she always puts too far to the right of an i. You press your thumb against it, hard enough to crease the page.

The lock clicks.

You push the chair under the door handle. It fits badly. Leila has told the landlord six times that the kitchen chairs are too wobbly to use safely. Tonight you would like them to be considerably less affordable.

“Sam,” the person outside says. “You left me out here.”

“You're wearing my coat.”

“So are you.”

You look down. The coat is over the chair at the table, exactly where you left it after school.

A little black paw appears outside the kitchen window and taps the glass.

You turn so quickly the notebook falls shut. On the fire escape sits a cat, fur puffed out against the cold. It has one pale whisker, a nick in an ear and the offended expression of an animal who has been waiting a long time.

It taps again.

You can hear the chair scraping across the floor behind you.

The window has been painted shut at the bottom. You hook your fingers under the frame and pull. Nothing. The cat mouths something through the glass. You stare at it.

It rolls its eyes, leans closer and says, distinctly, “Latch.”

You undo the latch.

The frame shoots upward. Cold air stings the wet inside of your nose. The cat climbs over the sill, shakes snow onto the chemistry worksheet, and pushes past you towards the notebook.

“Coat,” it says. “Phone. Shoes. Leave the laptop.”

You haven't mentioned the laptop.

“The expensive square,” the cat adds, impatiently. “Humans always go back for the expensive square.”

A screw falls from the door handle and bounces under the fridge.

You put on your coat. The real one, with the patch that catches on your thumb. You shove your feet into your boots, grab the notebook and phone, and climb through the window.

At the top of the fire escape, you look back.

Your door opens three inches against the chair. In the gap you see your own hand, then an eye. The person inside your apartment looks towards the window. Its mouth moves. You can't hear the words over the traffic below.

The cat bites your sleeve and pulls.

You follow it down.
