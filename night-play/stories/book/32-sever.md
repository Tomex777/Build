# A Record of the Missing

You take the unsent call into an empty part of the corridor. The hospital is beginning its evening routine. Visitors put on coats. Staff change shifts. Somebody wheels a cart past you and says excuse me twice before you notice you're in the way.

You sit down where you can see Mum's curtain.

The destination waits on the screen. Friday, 02:13. You remember what the voice said. You remember the terror of hearing yourself from a day that hadn't arrived. The reserved date on the line was never the date of the speaker. It was the next place the old receiving instruction could put a message.

Now you can give it the voice it was waiting for.

“Whatever happens,” you say when the call connects, “do not open the door.”

You hear yourself move in the kitchen. You give the instruction about the notebook. You start to say one more thing, and the line ends.

For a long time you look at the dark screen.

You want to believe the warning could make the ending different. That another version of you could find one more piece, persuade one more person, hold the chalk a little steadier. You cannot send a second call. The socket is closed.

You go back to Mum.

Three weeks later, you and Wren sit in the annex with a stack of blank cards. She names the voices she remembers. You write them down. Sometimes she knows a whole name and a sentence. Sometimes only a sound. You leave spaces instead of filling them with guesses.

The letter from Mrs. Bell lies beside the cards. Its address has faded but remains legible where Imani copied it. Whether you deliver it or keep it waiting is a decision the broken bell can no longer make for you.

Mum comes to help when her hand allows it. She is home now. The phrase still catches you by surprise. You hear her drop a spoon in the kitchen and feel relief before irritation can reach the sound.

Her return does not arrive as one clean happy thing. There are appointments, police interviews, questions from school and neighbours. She tires quickly. You get angry over small things because the large ones are hard to begin. Once you snap at her for moving your notebook, and she puts it back without telling you how lucky you are to have her there. Later, you apologise for the way you said it. You don't apologise for needing your things left alone.

Leila has started using a shared calendar. She calls it practical. You suspect it is also her way of letting you see the plans before they become instructions. You put your own appointments in, reluctantly. Neither of you celebrates this development out loud.

Wren is quieter than she was before the bell broke. She no longer changes faces when she is frightened. Instead she goes very still and asks people to stop talking for a minute. Usually they do. She is learning which people need reminding.

Julian's hand is recovering. He has told his sister about the wedding memory. She sent him a voice message three minutes long, mostly calling him an idiot and asking when he was coming round. He keeps it backed up in three places.

Imani has a decision from the selection coach. She tells you in person. You go to her next session and sit at the right end of the rink this time, where she can see you without turning her head too far.

Voss does not come to the annex. His part in Nadia's disappearance and the dangerous equipment is being investigated. You don't know whether any ordinary court can account for the rest. You know Leila gave them every record she could explain, including her own signature on the early tests.

One evening, Wren stops halfway through a name.

“What if I forget?” she asks.

You look at the cards. At the spaces you left.

“We'll keep what we've got.”

She nods, but she isn't entirely reassured. You don't add a prettier sentence to make yourself feel more helpful.

At home, Mum has burned the toast. The alarm is going off. Miso is shouting from the windowsill as if he has personally discovered a fire nobody else has noticed.

You open the kitchen window. Cold air enters. The alarm stops.

Mum stands in the middle of the room holding a plate with two black rectangles on it.

“I'm out of practice.”

“You were never good at toast.”

She looks offended for a second, then laughs. You put the plate in the sink and get out the cereal.

Your phone rings. A schoolfriend, asking whether you want the notes from chemistry. You look at Mum. She nods towards it and goes to fill the kettle.

You answer.

THE END
