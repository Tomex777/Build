# A Record of the Missing

I take the unsent call into an empty part of the corridor. The hospital is beginning its evening routine. Visitors put on coats. Staff change shifts. Somebody wheels a cart past me and says excuse me twice before I notice I'm in the way.

I sit down where I can see Mum's curtain.

The destination waits on the screen. Friday, 02:13. I remember what the voice said. I remember the terror of hearing myself from a day that hadn't arrived. The reserved date on the line was never the date of the speaker. It was the next place the old receiving instruction could put a message.

Now I can give it the voice it was waiting for.

“Whatever happens,” I say when the call connects, “do not open the door.”

I hear myself move in the kitchen. I give the instruction about the notebook. I start to say one more thing, and the line ends.

For a long time I look at the dark screen.

I want to believe the warning could make the ending different. That another version of me could find one more piece, persuade one more person, hold the chalk a little steadier. I cannot send a second call. The socket is closed.

I go back to Mum.

Three weeks later, Wren and I sit in the annex with a stack of blank cards. She names the voices she remembers. I write them down. Sometimes she knows a whole name and a sentence. Sometimes only a sound. I leave spaces instead of filling them with guesses.

The letter from Mrs. Bell lies beside the cards. Its address has faded but remains legible where Imani copied it. Whether I deliver it or keep it waiting is a decision the broken bell can no longer make for me.

Mum comes to help when her hand allows it. She is home now. The phrase still catches me by surprise. I hear her drop a spoon in the kitchen and feel relief before irritation can reach the sound.

Her return does not arrive as one clean happy thing. There are appointments, police interviews, questions from school and neighbours. She tires quickly. I get angry over small things because the large ones are hard to begin. Once I snap at her for moving my notebook, and she puts it back without telling me how lucky I am to have her there. Later, I apologise for the way I said it. I don't apologise for needing my things left alone.

Leila has started using a shared calendar. She calls it practical. I suspect it is also her way of letting me see the plans before they become instructions. I put my own appointments in, reluctantly. Neither of us celebrates this development out loud.

Wren is quieter than she was before the bell broke. She no longer changes faces when she is frightened. Instead she goes very still and asks people to stop talking for a minute. Usually they do. She is learning which people need reminding.

Julian's hand is recovering. He has told his sister about the wedding memory. She sent him a voice message three minutes long, mostly calling him an idiot and asking when he was coming round. He keeps it backed up in three places.

Imani has a decision from the selection coach. She tells me in person. I go to her next session and sit at the right end of the rink this time, where she can see me without turning her head too far.

Voss does not come to the annex. His part in Nadia's disappearance and the dangerous equipment is being investigated. I don't know whether any ordinary court can account for the rest. I know Leila gave them every record she could explain, including her own signature on the early tests.

One evening, Wren stops halfway through a name.

“What if I forget?” she asks.

I look at the cards. At the spaces I left.

“We'll keep what we've got.”

She nods, but she isn't entirely reassured. I don't add a prettier sentence to make myself feel more helpful.

At home, Mum has burned the toast. The alarm is going off. Miso is shouting from the windowsill as if he has personally discovered a fire nobody else has noticed.

I open the kitchen window. Cold air enters. The alarm stops.

Mum stands in the middle of the room holding a plate with two black rectangles on it.

“I'm out of practice.”

“You were never good at toast.”

She looks offended for a second, then laughs. I put the plate in the sink and get out the cereal.

My phone rings. A schoolfriend, asking whether I want the notes from chemistry. I look at Mum. She nods towards it and goes to fill the kettle.

I answer.

THE END
