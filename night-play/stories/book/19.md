# The Voice He Kept

The recording is not where Julian expects it to be. The index lists Eva Voss under a different surname, the one her mother used. He finds it because you copied the crossed-out number instead of only the replacement.

In the exchange room, the booth assigned to the original call is full of falling snow.

Not a picture of snow. Flakes drift against the glass and melt on the floor. Behind them is a kitchen with a yellow blind and a clock shaped like a plate. A telephone lies on the counter, its cord twisted around a chair.

Voss sits beside it. He looks younger, not because his face has fewer lines but because he hasn't yet learned how to hold it still. A woman stands by the sink. She is wearing a coat over pyjamas.

“This is the record?” you ask.

Julian nods. “Don't touch either of them. They can't hear us unless we enter the live end.”

You stand just inside the booth. Snow lands on your sleeve and disappears.

The phone rings. Voss reaches for it so quickly he knocks the chair over.

“Eva?”

At first there is only static. The woman at the sink takes one step towards him. He lifts a hand to stop her.

A child's voice says, “Dad?”

You know how this will end. You know he will hear that voice and spend the next year making other people carry what he cannot put down. Knowing doesn't make it easier to watch him close his eyes.

“I'm here,” he says. “I'm here, sweetheart.”

The woman puts a hand over her mouth.

Eva asks about a yellow bowl. You don't understand. Voss does. He says it is still on the shelf. She tells him she put a note underneath it for her mother and forgot to say.

The woman leaves the kitchen. You hear cupboard doors open in another room. When she comes back she is holding a folded piece of paper. She sits down on the floor beside the sink.

Voss keeps asking questions. Where is Eva? Who is with her? Can she see a door? She answers some and ignores others. Once she asks him to stop talking because she is trying to remember something.

He doesn't stop.

“Will you come home?” he asks.

There is a long silence. The snow in the booth falls more slowly.

“Dad. I don't think that's what it's for.”

He looks towards the control box beside the phone. You recognise the diagram on its lid.

“Just stay on the line.”

“I have to go.”

“No. Listen to me. Stay. I'll—”

The call ends.

Voss does not move the receiver from his ear. His wife is still sitting on the floor with the note in her hand. She looks up at him and says his name once.

He presses the callback button.

The booth goes dark.

You find yourself standing in the exchange room with snow melting inside your collar.

Imani says, “That's the real call.”

“Yes,” Julian says.

“The thing in the flat isn't her.”

“Not according to this record.”

You touch the notebook under your arm. The voice said Mum in your kitchen. It knew her cough, her impatient listen, the way she used your name. Voss had one true call. How many false ones would you have answered to hear yours again?

Julian copies the acceptance sequence from the booth's control. It isn't a spell that makes a person obey. It records a permission given for one message, with a clear end. When the caller leaves, the receiving side must let the connection close.

“He kept reopening it,” you say.

Miso sits under the desk, collar almost hidden by his fur.

“You don't get the same person just because you use the same number.”

You save a transcript of the final exchange in the notebook. Imani asks whether you will show Voss.

“I don't know.”

“He might need to hear it.”

“He heard it the first time.”

She considers that without trying to improve it.

On the booth's inside wall, under the fresh layer of snow, a small handprint appears. Then a second. Someone is pressing against the glass from a place you cannot see.

A girl's voice speaks from the disconnected phone.

“Did she like the bowl?”

You turn.

The voice is lighter than Eva's. The question sounds rehearsed.

“I don't know,” you say.

“He wants me to know.”

Imani steps closer to you. Julian reaches for the booth door, then stops with his good hand just above the handle.

You ask, “What's your name?”

The snow stops falling.

After a while, the voice says, “I haven't got that right yet.”
