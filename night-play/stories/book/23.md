# What You Said Before

The reader works on a piece of string. It works on a paper connection. It works on an empty telephone circuit Julian builds from two handsets and a coil of cable Dennis finds in a cupboard.

It fails when Wren puts one finger against the brass socket.

Every light in the storeroom goes out. Leila holds the bell case shut while Julian pulls the contact free. In the sudden dark, somebody knocks a glass off the table. You hear it break near your feet.

“Don't move,” Imani says.

The emergency lights come on. Wren is standing by the door. The borrowed gloves lie empty on the floor.

Julian looks at the reader's indicator. It has counted three thousand four hundred and eleven responses in less than a second.

“That isn't possible,” he says.

Wren backs out of the room.

You follow her into the corridor before anyone can stop you. She is pressed against the wall beside the vending machine, looking down at her hands.

“I said I didn't know,” she says.

“I know.”

“Then stop looking like that.”

You don't know what your face is doing. You make yourself look at the machine instead. Someone has put a packet of crisps in the wrong spiral, and two flavours are wedged together behind the glass.

“I thought we had it,” you say.

“You thought you had me.”

The sentence stings because you can hear the possible truth in it. You had started thinking of Wren as the missing part of the circuit. Not the person who couldn't work out a pair of gloves.

Imani comes out with a dustpan. She sets it against the wall and stands between you, leaving room for Wren to move away.

“We're going to need a different way to count,” she says.

Julian appears in the doorway. “We don't have time to rebuild a whole—”

“Then don't start by telling her she's impossible.”

He closes his mouth.

Leila joins you a minute later. Her palm is red where the case burned through the cloth. She has wrapped it in a damp towel.

“We need the original receiving record,” she says. “The part that establishes who is answering. There should be one for every person the line can name.”

“Wren doesn't have one,” Julian says.

“Then we ask how to make one.”

“Where?”

He knows the answer before your aunt gives it. His face changes.

“No.”

“The witness office.”

“No. The last time I asked somebody down there to fix a record, they took a year of my life I can describe but can't remember.”

“Then we don't give them an undefined payment,” Leila says.

“You make it sound like I forgot to read a form.”

Your aunt goes quiet. The correction lands hard enough that you see her accept it before she answers.

“I didn't mean that.”

“I know. You still said it.”

For a while nobody has a useful next sentence.

Imani takes you into the empty stairwell. You think she wants help moving something, until she closes the fire door behind you.

“Tell me the whole plan.”

“We don't have one yet.”

“Then the whole problem.”

You lean against the handrail. It is cold through the sleeve of your coat.

You tell her what Julian said about the messages entering one person. You tell her how simple it seemed, for a second, to stand where Mum was standing. She doesn't interrupt. When you've finished, she sits down on a stair.

“You were going to swap yourself.”

“I thought about it.”

“And everyone else carries on deciding how to fix things, except now there's another person stuck.”

“You don't know that.”

“No. Neither do you.”

You sit on the step above her. From the storeroom comes the muffled sound of Leila and Julian moving the table.

She takes a deep breath. “I can be your friend without agreeing to everything.”

You look at her. She seems annoyed at having to say friend out loud. You are grateful enough to keep your own face turned towards the rail.

“Okay,” you say.

“Okay isn't a plan.”

“No. But I heard you.”

She studies you for a moment, then gets up. You pick the dustpan up on the way back. There is still broken glass in the room, and somebody needs to clear it before the next emergency.
