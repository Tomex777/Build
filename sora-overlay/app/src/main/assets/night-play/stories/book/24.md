# The Witness Office

Anubis meets me at the bottom of the concrete staircase. He is carrying the driver's cap under one arm. Without it, he looks taller.

Miso stops two steps above him.

“We need a receiving record,” I say.

“So I was told.”

“By who?”

“Someone who does not enjoy being called from a laundry basement.”

I think of the three pens and decide not to ask whether gods complain about each other in a group chat.

Leila has stayed at the annex with Dennis and the bell. Julian came as far as the staircase and stopped. I found him standing with his good hand on the rail, looking at the place where the concrete becomes stone.

“I'll wait here,” he said.

I told him where I was going and when I would return. He checked the time himself. Neither of us called it cowardice.

Now I follow Anubis down a corridor lined with doors. Behind one, someone is grinding grain. Behind another, a woman and a man argue over the ownership of a boat. Nothing resembles the endless fire I expected from the word underworld when I first heard it in a classroom.

At the far end is an office with two chairs. A scale stands against the wall. It is small and practical, its pans scratched from use.

I stop in the doorway.

Anubis notices.

“This isn't the judgment of the dead. Nobody is weighing your heart. Sit down.”

I do. Imani remains standing. Wren stays beside the door with her hands in the pockets of the coat Leila found for her. The sleeves reach past her fingers.

A woman enters from the other room. She wears a plain white dress with a narrow blue border. There is a feather on the desk beside her, its shaft worn smooth by handling.

“You have brought a person whose record is attached to several thousand senders,” she says.

Wren looks down.

“Do you want that record separated?” the woman asks her.

Wren looks at me. I make myself remain silent.

“I want to know what happens after.”

“Good. Then that is the first question.”

The woman places three blank cards on the desk. On one she writes sender. On one, message. On the third, receiver. She puts a pebble on each.

“When the circuit operates as intended, these remain distinguishable. The message passes. The sender can leave. The receiver can refuse.”

She places the pebbles together.

“Someone has made refusal count as continued attendance. Over time the messages have begun using one another to remain legible.”

“Wren,” I say.

“Is the person who has grown in those connections. That doesn't mean she owns everyone she can hear.”

Wren's hands come out of the coat pockets.

“I didn't ask for them.”

“No,” the woman says. “You didn't.”

I hear Mum's recording in my head. The room, the second reader, the problem of recognising who is actually answering.

“Can she survive without them?”

The woman turns the third card over. On its back is an empty box.

“A receiving record establishes a person independently of what they carry. But it requires the person to claim it. It cannot be issued to Voss's daughter on behalf of somebody who isn't Voss's daughter.”

Wren looks at the box.

“He says names have to be old.”

“People say many convenient things about names.”

Anubis stands at the wall, giving nobody help they haven't asked for. I want him to tell me what to do. I suspect he knows this.

The woman holds out the blank card to Wren.

“What name do you ask us to record?”

“Wren.”

“And whose permission brought you here?”

She looks at me again. Then at Imani, who has been waiting quietly beside her.

“Mine.”

The woman writes the name. The pebble on the third card separates from the others.

Wren gasps. For one second her face becomes empty, too smooth to read. Imani takes a step towards her and stops before touching.

“Can I?” she asks.

Wren nods. Imani takes her hand.

The features return slowly. I recognise the face she chose under the streetlight. Her mouth trembles with effort.

“There are still too many,” she says.

“The record gives you somewhere to stand,” the woman says. “It doesn't remove the people holding the road closed.”

She hands me a copy of the receiving sequence, but gives Wren the original card. I don't reach for it. Wren presses it against her chest as if she has only just found out where to keep something important.

On the way out, I ask Anubis whether the woman was Ma'at.

He looks at me for a while.

“You met someone doing the work of her office. It may be enough to remember the work.”

At the staircase, Julian is still there. He has brought the trolley up from the landing because he couldn't stand waiting without doing anything.

Wren shows him the card.

He looks at it and then at her. Very carefully, he says, “Hello, Wren.”

She grins. It changes her whole face.
