# The Hand on the Cable

Beyond the doors, the lower station opens around me. It is larger than it was during the first rescue, or I am arriving from a side I didn't see. Rows of switchboards rise towards an arch of dark water. Telephone cables hang between stone columns. Every cable carries a brass label.

The labels are names.

Some are written in letters I recognise. Some use scripts I cannot read. A few are only marks, as if the person writing them had almost forgotten the shape before reaching the end.

Julian stops beside one, not touching it.

“Those aren't all dead callers,” he says.

Leila looks at the label. She recognises the name.

“It's a patient. From the first tests. She declined.”

Voss says nothing.

I turn towards him. “You kept them.”

“I kept the routing information. I didn't send—”

“The line can't tell the difference,” Julian says.

I look down the long rows of names. People waiting for a call. People who refused one. People who once loved someone who used a system that couldn't bear an empty space. The bypass has turned a record into a place to hold them.

Wren presses her hands over her ears.

“They're louder here.”

Imani takes her to the edge of a table, away from the nearest cables. Julian sets up the reader. Leila checks the receiving sequence against the original card. I follow Miso towards the inner room.

Mum is standing this time. She has wrapped a strip of cloth around her hand, but it has burned through in several places.

When she sees me, her face closes with fear before relief can reach it.

“No. {{firstName}}, I told you—”

“I listened. Then we found the other reader.”

“Who came with you?”

I tell her. Her eyes reach Voss before I finish.

He stops in the doorway.

“I have the revised sequence,” he says.

Mum looks at the bag, the brass box, Julian's grey hand. She understands more from those objects than I could have explained.

Then she looks back at me.

“You've been learning.”

“You left notes.”

“Not enough.”

“No.”

She accepts the answer. It costs her something; I can see it. I don't soften it before she has time to hear it.

I take her free hand again. This time I don't need to check whether the familiar rough patch is there. Her thumb closes over mine.

“I wanted to call,” she says.

“I know.”

“No. I need to tell you this part properly. I tried. Then I stopped. Every time I thought I could make the next attempt safer, I found another reason to wait. At first it was the right decision. After a while I couldn't tell how much was right and how much was being afraid to hear you ask me to come home.”

“You could have let me ask.”

“Yes.”

Her voice breaks on the word. I look at the burned cloth around her other hand. I am angry with her and afraid for her at the same time, and neither feeling makes room for the other.

Imani comes to the doorway. “We need to put the reader close enough to connect without pulling the hand control.”

Mum wipes her face with her sleeve. “Under the table. There's a shelf. Don't touch the left bracket.”

The instructions give her a way to stand upright again.

For ten minutes I work around her. Julian positions the reader. Imani passes the connectors in the order I rehearsed. Leila renews the holding marks without closing the release side. Miso moves along the floor, checking where the cables pass into the stone.

Voss stands by the master switch. He has the task he asked for and no private line to operate. I watch him whenever his hands move.

Wren comes in last. Mum looks at her face, then at the card she is holding.

“Hello,” she says.

Wren doesn't answer at once.

“You called me the Unnamed.”

“I didn't know what else to call what I was hearing.”

“Wren.”

Mum nods. “Hello, Wren.”

Wren moves closer. “You kept saying {{firstName}}.”

“I know.”

“I thought I could bring you there.”

Mum looks at me, then down at the cable in her hand. “That was kind. It was also dangerous.”

Wren's mouth twists. “People can do both?”

“Yes,” Mum says. “I'm finding that very inconvenient.”

It is not a grand explanation. Wren seems to prefer it.

Julian checks the reader. The first indicator glows. The second remains dark.

“We need the actual receiving mark,” he says. “Nadia's. The one Adrian copied.”

Mum nods towards the back of the panel. “His version's there. Mine is underneath.”

I kneel and find the two layers. The false signature is visible on top. Beneath it, scratched into the brass, is the mark my mother drew before the bypass existed.

I copy it exactly. The final stroke curves left instead of right. A small error to build eleven months around.

The second indicator comes on.

Leila calls Dennis through the outer line.

“Connected,” she says.

The timer starts.
