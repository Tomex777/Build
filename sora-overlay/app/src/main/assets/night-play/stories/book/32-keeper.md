# A Way to Call Home

I check the return line against Mum's manual before using it. One message, back to the point where the first claim began. The date is a reserved receiving slot, a place in the route rather than a promise about which day the speaker belongs to.

It explains the wrong date on my phone. It does not make hearing my own breathing less strange.

“Whatever happens,” I say, “do not open the door.”

I give myself the notebook. The kitchen drawer. I start one last sentence and the line closes before I know how to finish it.

Miso lifts his head from the blanket.

“Done?”

“I think so.”

“Then somebody's waiting.”

I turn to the next light.

The first week is hard in ways I didn't rehearse. I miss being able to leave a room because I am tired of it. I miss food I can complain about. I miss the sound the flat's radiator makes before it warms up.

I do not miss school until Julian brings my chemistry work through the controlled line. Then I miss the whole building, including the corridor where the lockers never close properly.

Mum calls every evening. Sometimes she talks about the receiving point she is trying to build. Sometimes she tells me what she bought at the supermarket. When she begins explaining an experiment without asking how I am, I interrupt her. The first time, she is hurt. The second time, she catches herself.

Leila keeps the calls from becoming only research. She asks about sleep, then listens when I say I don't know whether I am sleeping or merely resting between messages. She writes it down without pretending the answer is reassuring.

Imani brings a chair. Getting it through the controlled route takes most of a Saturday and an argument with a door which appears not to recognise furniture as an authorised visitor. She refuses to leave it behind. When it finally arrives, the seat is scratched but intact.

“Sit,” she says.

I sit. It is an excellent chair.

She stands beside me for a while, looking at the board. A light comes on, then another. I let them wait. That is permitted now.

“I got the place,” she says.

“At the selection?”

She nods.

I get up so quickly the chair scrapes backwards. She laughs, then I am hugging her, and for a minute the station contains a celebration which has nothing to do with being brave.

Wren visits with Julian. She is learning to write messages which belong only to her. Most are questions. Some are complaints about how many ordinary words have more than one meaning. She brings Miso a knitted mouse. He ignores it until she leaves, then carries it to the blanket.

Voss has not tried to call. The investigation continues above. Nadia has supplied the original manuals and signed a statement about her own work. Julian tells me the records will be complicated. I believe him. I have developed a strong dislike of stories which end when the person responsible looks sad enough.

I help a few callers each day. I turn down requests that would make somebody else answer by force. I ask the right question before accepting a message. Sometimes a receiver refuses. The refusal is recorded, and the line closes. No one is punished for it.

At the end of the third week, Mum reaches the station through the visitor route. Not a voice. A person. She has a bag of sandwiches and several pages of calculations held together by a bulldog clip.

She puts the calculations down first.

“How are you?” she asks.

I look at her hand. It is healing. I look at the sandwiches, the ordinary grease showing through the paper.

“Angry,” I say. “Sometimes. Homesick.”

She sits in the old chair, which Imani repaired rather than throwing away.

“Tell me.”

So I do. Not everything. Enough to begin.

Later, she shows me the calculations. There may be a way to divide the station's role among several consenting operators, each with a defined shift and a way out. It is not ready. It is not a promise she can safely make. She tells me both facts before asking whether I want to help test it.

I read the first page. Make a correction in the margin. Ask a question.

Mum smiles at the familiar impatience in my voice.

On the switchboard, a light comes on. I check its conditions, then lift the receiver.

“Hello,” I say.

Miso closes his eyes in the warm patch beside my new chair. Mum opens her notebook. Somewhere above me, people are having an ordinary evening, and the controlled line home remains open.

THE END
