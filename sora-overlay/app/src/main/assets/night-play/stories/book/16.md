# What Leila Signed

The flat is colder than I remember. Leila goes straight to the radiator and turns the valve, then checks the mirror by the front door before allowing anyone to take off a coat.

Imani goes to the kitchen. Julian checks the window latch. Miso stays on the threshold, making a point of waiting until I look at him.

“You're coming in,” I say.

He steps over without answering.

Mum's manuals are on the bottom shelf in the sitting room. I used to build little walls around them with my schoolbooks because she complained when I stacked anything heavy on top. Most are ordinary repair guides, full of parts diagrams and warnings about electricity. The one I need has no title on its spine.

Inside, someone has written a list of names. I recognise Nadia, Adrian, Celia. The fourth is Leila's.

I bring the book into the kitchen.

My aunt has filled the kettle. She turns it off before it boils.

“I witnessed the first tests,” she says.

“Of course you did.”

She looks at the open page instead of defending herself.

“There were people at the hospital who died before someone could get there. Nadia wanted to know whether something could be done about that.”

“You helped.”

“Yes.”

“Did they know?”

“Some did. We asked. We wrote it down. Not everybody said yes.”

I turn the page. Beside several names, in my aunt's compact handwriting, is the word declined. No explanation. No attempt to talk them round.

“What happened to the noes?”

“Nothing. That was the point.”

Julian sets his bandaged hand on the table. Leila looks at it and closes her eyes briefly.

“Adrian wanted the line to stay open after a refusal. He said someone might change their mind. Nadia said they could make a new call.”

“And you?”

“I thought a delay could be harmless if nobody received anything until they agreed.”

I wait. She rubs one thumb against the other.

“I signed off on a delay. I didn't ask enough about what he did with it.”

Miso sits beneath the chair nearest her. He doesn't look comfortable.

I keep turning pages. The manual shows two identical sockets facing each other. One is labelled CALL. The other is labelled RECEIVE. In between, a line returns to the caller before the circuit closes.

The bypass crosses that line.

Julian leans over my shoulder.

“It never asks again. It records the first contact as continuing authority.”

My aunt nods. “When Nadia found out, she tried to shut it down from inside. She thought she could put the original check back.”

“She told you?”

“She told me after she went.”

The kettle clicks as it cools. Nobody moves towards it.

“I got one call,” Leila says. “She said she was handling it. She sounded annoyed with herself. I was annoyed with her too. I told her to stop trying to fix everything alone.”

“What did she say?”

“She asked me to stay with you until she got back.”

I look down at the list of declined names. My aunt has made the same mistake as my mother: turning a request into an arrangement that keeps lasting because nobody knows how to end it.

“You should've told me,” I say.

“I should have told you enough to understand why I didn't have an answer.”

“That's a very careful sentence.”

“Yes.”

I put the manual down. My hands have started trembling, not dramatically, just enough to make the paper rustle. I hate it. I hold the edge of the table until it stops.

Imani opens a cupboard, finds mugs and lines four of them beside the kettle. She doesn't ask whether anyone wants tea. The practical interruption gives me somewhere to put my eyes.

Julian studies the socket diagram.

“We need another reader. Independent. Not a replacement body.”

Miso says, “Adrian still has the original.”

I look at him.

He lowers his head and scratches at the collar. The brass plate makes a thin sound against his claws.

“I can tell you where he stores things,” he says. “I can't promise the things are there.”

Leila turns a page back and points to a smaller diagram I nearly missed. It shows how to release a connection without cutting through it. Two marks, one inside the other, followed by an open end.

“I'm going to teach you this,” she says. “Then you're going to teach it back to me before you try it on anything.”

I sit down. She gives me a scrap of paper and draws the first line slowly.

It takes almost an hour. I get impatient twice. She gets impatient once and apologises before I have to point it out. Imani practises with a piece of string. Julian records the sequence in words as well as pictures.

Miso watches from the chair. When the string loosens without breaking, he looks away.

I keep the scrap of paper. On the back, under the grocery list already written there, I put the date and the word release.
