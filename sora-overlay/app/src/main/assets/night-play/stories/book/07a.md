# An Address Still in Use

The letter has an address on a street Julian says isn't far from the exchange room's ordinary exit. It is still early enough that the shops have their shutters down. I stand under an awning while a delivery driver drags crates into a bakery and wonder whether showing up at a stranger's house before seven counts as a worse idea when the letter comes from his dead mother.

Imani checks the note in her phone.

“We can leave it. We promised to find out whether he lives there. Not make him open it.”

Julian is carrying the evidence box against his chest. He has been looking at the time every few minutes.

“Your aunt thinks we're on our way back.”

“So tell her where we are,” Imani says.

I do. Leila replies with an address confirmation and a request to stay together. No lecture. It is almost more difficult to argue with.

The house has a small front garden full of snow-covered pots. Two bins stand by the side gate. A light is on in the kitchen. Through the glass beside the door I can see a coat rack with far more coats than the space was designed to hold.

I ring once.

A man in slippers opens the door. He looks at the three of us, then at the box, then at Miso. His hand stays on the latch.

“Yes?”

“Martin Bell?”

“Who's asking?”

Fair question. I give my first name and explain that the letter was found among papers belonging to his mother. It is a careful explanation. I don't enjoy how much it resembles Leila's earlier ones.

He looks at the envelope.

“My mother died in November.”

“We know.”

He takes it, examines the handwriting and leans one shoulder against the door frame. For a moment he seems too tired to keep being suspicious.

“Where was this?”

“In an archive,” Julian says. “She worked as a volunteer operator. We're cataloguing a group of personal papers.”

Not entirely true. Not entirely false. Martin looks at the box again and accepts enough of it to stop asking at the door.

He doesn't invite me in. I am glad. It means I don't have to explain which part of my reason for being there is a trespass into his life.

He turns the envelope over. The back is unsealed.

“Did you read it?”

“No,” I say.

I could have. In the exchange room, during the long wait for the door. In Dennis's car. On the pavement. The notebook has been full of things I needed to know, and it would have been easy to put the letter in the same category.

Martin nods once. He opens it while I stand on the step. His eyes move quickly over the first lines, then stop.

Behind him, someone in the kitchen asks whether he needs help. He says no without looking away from the page.

I feel I should leave. Nobody has told me to leave. I have become very aware of the wet soles of my boots and the small stone under one heel.

Then Martin folds the letter.

“She kept telling me not to drive in the snow,” he says.

I don't answer. I know something he hasn't asked me to know, and saying it would make me the owner of a conversation that belongs to him.

He looks at the envelope again.

“I was angry. I thought she didn't want help. She always made it sound like a nuisance, having somebody come over.”

Imani shifts beside me. Julian studies a crack in the step.

Martin rubs his thumb along the fold.

“Thank you for bringing it.”

“You're welcome.”

He goes inside. The door closes gently. I hear a chair move in the kitchen, then another voice, too low to understand.

On the pavement, I take out my notebook to mark the promise complete. My pen hovers above the page.

Julian says, “Don't write what he said.”

“I wasn't.”

I had been about to. Not from malice. It was a clue, a response, something that might help explain the system. I close the notebook and put it away.

Miso walks beside the garden wall. His paws make four precise prints in the new snow. I watch them until the next patch of cleared pavement makes them disappear.

At the corner, Imani buys three small buns from the bakery. Julian says he doesn't want one, then eats half before remembering he said that. I stand under the awning, eating warm bread while the delivery driver restacks his empty crates.

“Was it worth the time?” Julian asks.

I think of Mum in a room I haven't reached yet. The deadline. The narrow places a day can acquire when I'm afraid of using it badly.

“I don't know how to count it.”

He accepts that. I am grateful he doesn't tell me the answer should be obvious.

On my phone, the routing entry for Celia Bell changes. A small line of text appears beneath it: received without continuation.

I show Julian. He stops eating.

“That,” he says, “is what the acknowledgement is supposed to look like.”

I write down those words. Not Martin's letter. Not his grief. Just the part the line has given me permission to see.

When I reach the annex, Leila is standing outside the door. She looks at the time, at the three of us, and then at the bag from the bakery.

“Any left?”

Imani hands her the last bun. My aunt eats half of it before asking for the report.
